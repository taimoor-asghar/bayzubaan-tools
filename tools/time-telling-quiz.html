Two clocks: official vs colloquial

German has two parallel systems for telling the time, and mixing them up is the fastest way to confuse a native speaker. The official style (offizielle Uhrzeit) uses the 24-hour clock and reads the digits: 14:30 is vierzehn Uhr dreißig, 8:05 is acht Uhr fünf. You'll hear it in train announcements, appointment confirmations, and news broadcasts. The colloquial style (umgangssprachlich) uses the 12-hour clock with viertel, halb, nach, and vor: 14:30 in conversation is halb drei. Everyday speech almost always uses the colloquial form — answering "vierzehn Uhr dreißig" when a friend asks the time sounds as stiff as saying "fourteen thirty hours" in English. The translator above shows both, so you can match the register to the situation.

The halb trap: half TO the hour

This is the single most-missed item in German time vocabulary, and the quiz drills it hard. Halb drei does not mean 3:30 — it means 2:30. Halb names the hour that is half arrived: at halb drei, half of the third hour has passed. English speakers default to "half past three", which maps to the wrong German. The fix: whenever you hear halb X, subtract one hour from X — halb acht is 7:30, halb eins is 12:30, halb vier is 3:30. Drill this until the subtraction is automatic, because in conversation there's no time to compute. (Note: in English the "half past" convention has the same historical logic, but modern speakers think "half past the current hour" — German never moved off the original meaning.)

Viertel nach and viertel vor

The quarters are straightforward once halb is internalised: viertel nach = quarter past (7:15 → viertel nach sieben), viertel vor = quarter to (8:45 → viertel vor neun). Like halb, viertel vor names the coming hour. A southern-German wrinkle: in Bavaria and Austria you'll also hear viertel drei for 2:15 and dreiviertel drei for 2:45 — literally "a quarter (of the way to) three" and "three-quarters three". These are regional, never wrong in the South, but incomprehensible in Hamburg. For learners, stick to viertel nach/vor, understand the southern forms passively, and recognise them when travelling.

Nach and vor for the other minutes

Between the quarter marks, colloquial German anchors to the nearest half hour. Minutes 1–29 use nach the current hour: 11:05 → fünf nach elf, 4:20 → zwanzig nach vier, 10:10 → zehn nach zehn. Minutes 31–59 use vor the next hour: 9:50 → zehn vor zehn, 10:40 → zwanzig vor elf, 6:55 → fünf vor sieben. English speakers tend to read "9:50" as "nine fifty" and reach for the official form — but in conversation, zehn vor zehn is what a native would say. Advanced forms like fünf vor halb zehn (9:25) and fünf nach halb drei (2:35) exist and are common, but they're built on the same logic; master the basics first, then absorb these by listening.

Ein Uhr, not eins Uhr

One o'clock is ein Uhr, never "eins Uhr". This is the same ein/eins split from the numbers drill: eins is the counting word ("eins, zwei, drei"), ein is the article-like form used in compounds and before nouns — and Uhr behaves like a feminine noun here. All other hours take the plain number: zwei Uhr, drei Uhr … zwölf Uhr. For midnight, colloquial German usually says Mitternacht rather than null Uhr (which is strictly official), and midday is mittag in context, though zwölf Uhr is unambiguous.

When official time is mandatory

Use the 24-hour official form for anything written or scheduled: appointments (Der Termin ist um vierzehn Uhr dreißig), train and flight times, opening hours, medical shift handovers — the Fachsprachenprüfung domain where misreading 14:30 as "halb drei"-casual could genuinely confuse a colleague. Note that Germans write 2:30pm as 14:30 Uhr (with Uhr after the digits) and say vierzehn Uhr dreißig. The 12-hour colloquial form is ambiguous by design — halb drei could be 2:30am or 2:30pm — so context carries the disambiguation, exactly as in English. In writing, the 24-hour form removes all doubt.

Prepositions of time: um, am, im

Telling the time grammatically means attaching the right preposition. Clock times take um: um halb acht, um viertel nach zwei. Days take am: am Montag, am dritten März (the date drill covers this). Months, seasons, and years take in (usually im): im Mai, im Sommer, im Jahr 2026 — or bare 2026 without a preposition. The classic learner error is "in halb acht" or "at halb acht" thinking; the trio um/am/im is a small closed set — memorise which time unit takes which, and clock-time sentences become automatic.

Regional and register notes

Beyond the southern viertel drei / dreiviertel drei, a few more variants exist. In parts of Austria, halb can combine with quarter logic in ways that surprise northerners — but you'll be understood with standard forms everywhere. Young speakers increasingly use the official form even in speech ("vierzehn Uhr dreißig") under the influence of digital clocks, especially in professional settings. And zwo sometimes replaces zwei in spoken times for clarity on bad phone lines (zwo Uhr zwanzig) — charming, informal, never written.

Study strategy

Start with the translator: set the time to :30 for each hour 1–12 and read the colloquial form aloud until halb names the coming hour without calculation. Then do the :15/:45 pairs, then random minutes. The quiz's distractors target the two classic failures — halb pointing the wrong way ("halb sieben" for 7:30) and slipping into official form mid-conversation ("sieben Uhr dreißig") — so a perfect score means both traps are closed. Finally, practise in real life: narrate your day in German time ("Ich stehe um halb sieben auf, der Zug fährt um viertel vor acht"), and read German train-station displays as official time while converting them to colloquial in your head. When both directions are instant, this topic is done.

Frequently asked questions

Why is 2:30 'halb drei' and not 'halb zwei'?

Halb names the hour that is half ARRIVED: at 2:30, half of the third hour has passed, so it's halb drei. Always subtract one from the named hour — halb acht is 7:30, halb eins is 12:30.

When should I use official vs colloquial time?

Official (14:30 → vierzehn Uhr dreißig, 24-hour clock) for announcements, timetables, appointments, and writing. Colloquial (halb drei, viertel nach zwei, 12-hour clock) for everyday conversation. Answering a friend with the official form sounds stiff.

What does 'viertel nach sieben' mean?

7:15 — 'a quarter past seven'. In the South you may also hear 'viertel acht' for the same time; stick to viertel nach for standard German, and understand the southern form passively.

Is it 'ein Uhr' or 'eins Uhr'?

Ein Uhr. Eins is only the standalone counting word; before Uhr (treated like a feminine noun) the form is ein — the same ein/eins split as in einundzwanzig vs eins.

What's the difference between 'viertel vor neun' and 'dreiviertel neun'?

Both mean 8:45. Viertel vor neun is standard everywhere; dreiviertel neun ('three-quarters nine', naming the coming hour) is used in Bavaria and Austria. Learners should use viertel vor and just recognise the southern form.
