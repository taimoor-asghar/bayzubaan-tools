From the top 120 to the next hundred

The first tool in this series covered the 120 most frequent German words — the core that unlocks basic understanding. This quiz moves you into ranks 121 to 220 of the frequency list: still extremely high-value vocabulary, but now the words get more concrete. Where the top 120 was dominated by articles, pronouns and helper verbs, this batch is full of everyday life: der Tisch, die Küche, bezahlen, verstehen, krank, pünktlich. These are the words you need for shopping, small talk, travel and daily routines — the situations where beginners actually use German.

Notice how the vocabulary shifts by category. The verbs here are almost all separable or prefixed — anfangen, aufhören, aufstehen, ausziehen, einkaufen — which is no coincidence: prefix verbs are the engine of everyday German, and mastering their main-clause splitting (Ich stehe um sieben auf) is what makes you sound natural rather than textbook. The adjectives cluster around states and judgements — müde, hungrig, interessant, gefährlich — exactly the words you reach for when describing your day.

How spaced repetition works (and why this quiz uses it)

Memory decays on a curve: without review, a new word fades within days. Spaced repetition flattens that curve by re-testing you at increasing intervals, right before you would forget. This quiz implements the core mechanic: any word you miss is pushed back into the question pool, so you meet it again later in the same session — and the end-of-quiz review list tells you exactly which words to carry into tomorrow's session.

The classic implementation is the Leitner box system: words live in boxes, correct answers promote them to a less-frequent box, mistakes demote them back to daily review. You can run this with paper flashcards or any spaced-repetition app — but the principle is the same as what this quiz does automatically. The single most important rule: never mark a word "known" from recognition alone. If you cannot produce the meaning from the German word cold, it is not learned yet.

Two directions, one word

This quiz deliberately tests in both directions. German → English checks recognition: can you understand the word when you read or hear it? English → German checks production: can you find the word when you need it? Production is always harder, and it is the skill you need for speaking. Most learners are shocked by the gap — a word you "know" one way can draw a blank the other way. That is normal, and it is exactly what the quiz is measuring.

Watch for the distractor design too: wrong options are drawn from the same word type, so you cannot guess from grammar. Bezahlt will not save you — you must actually know the meaning. When you miss, read the full example sentence in the feedback, not just the translation: the sentence shows you the word's frame (its preposition, its case, its typical position), which is half of really knowing a word.

Small traps in this batch

A few words here deserve a warning label. Bekommen is not in this batch, but gefallen is — and English speakers constantly mistranslate it: Das Buch gefällt mir means "I like the book", with the book as the subject and mir in the dative. Similarly fehlen in Du fehlst mir means "I miss you" — literally "you are missing to me". Learn these as whole patterns, not word-by-word.

Schmecken (in Das Essen schmeckt gut) takes a dative experiencer too: Es schmeckt mir. And kosten looks like "cost" and mostly is — but Was kostet das? is the single most useful shopping sentence in German, worth memorising whole. Finally, sicher spans "sure", "safe" and "secure" — Bist du sicher? (are you sure?) versus ein sicherer Ort (a safe place). Context disambiguates; the quiz distractors will train exactly that.

Two more traps worth naming. Ausziehen means both "to take off" (clothes: Zieh die Schuhe aus) and "to move out" (Wir ziehen nächste Woche aus) — the context decides, and mixing them up produces memorable misunderstandings. And sich erinnern is reflexive with an + Akkusativ (Ich erinnere mich an dich); English speakers often drop the reflexive pronoun or the preposition, producing ungrammatical sentences. Learn it as one chunk: sich erinnern an. The same chunking advice applies to sich entscheiden für and sich anziehen — German reflexive verbs are memorised with their pronouns, never without.

Frequently asked questions

What is spaced repetition and why does it work?

Memory fades on a curve; re-testing a word just before you would forget flattens that curve. This quiz re-queues every word you miss, so you are re-tested exactly where you are weakest — the core mechanic of systems like Leitner boxes and Anki.

Why test in both directions?

German-to-English tests recognition (understanding); English-to-German tests production (speaking and writing). Production is harder and usually lags behind — the quiz measures both so the gap becomes visible and trainable.

How many rounds should I do per day?

One 20-round session daily is enough. When you consistently score 18+/20, the batch is learned — move on, and rerun this quiz weekly as maintenance so the words stay active.

What should I do with words I keep missing?

Make a personal flashcard for each: German word on one side, meaning plus your own example sentence on the other. Writing the sentence yourself is a powerful memory event, and personal context sticks better than textbook examples.

Why are the wrong answers always the same word type?

So you cannot guess from grammar. If the distractors were random, you could eliminate nouns when asked for a verb. Same-type distractors force you to actually know the meaning — which is the whole point.

I know the top 120 already — is this the right next step?

Yes. Ranks 121–220 are the next most useful words by real-world frequency, shifting toward concrete everyday vocabulary (home, food, travel, daily routines). After ~220 words you will recognise a large share of simple German texts.
