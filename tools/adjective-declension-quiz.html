    Spot the table first, then the ending

    The decisive skill isn't knowing the tables — it's knowing which table you're in within a second of seeing the noun phrase. Ask one question: what comes before the adjective? der/die/das (or dieser, jeder, welcher) → weak: default -en, with -e in only five slots. ein/eine (or mein, dein, kein) → mixed: the three showcase endings guter / gute / gutes where the article can't show the case, -en everywhere else. Nothing → strong: the adjective carries the full case ending itself. Train this one-second classification and the endings follow automatically.

    Walkthrough: how to solve any sentence

    Take Ich trinke ein ___ Wein. Step 1: what precedes the adjective? ein → mixed. Step 2: gender and case? Wein is masculine, and as the thing being drunk it's accusative. Step 3: mixed table, masculine accusative → einen already shows the case, so the adjective relaxes to -en: einen guten Wein. Now ___ Wasser ist kalt: nothing precedes the adjective → strong; Wasser is neuter nominative → -es: kaltes Wasser. And mit ___ Milch: nothing precedes → strong; Milch is feminine dative (mit takes dative) → -er: mit kalter Milch. Three questions, three endings — every time.

    The trap sentences (and why they exist)

    Certain combinations are engineered by textbooks precisely because they break the -en autopilot. Ein guter Wein (masculine nominative mixed) vs. einen guten Wein (masculine accusative mixed): same article family, different showcase. Mit kalter Milch (strong feminine dative) vs. mit der kalten Milch (weak): the presence or absence of the article flips the ending from -er to -en. Gute Freunde (strong plural nominative, no article) vs. die guten Freunde (weak): same flip. These pairs are the highest-value things to drill, because they force you to run the classification step instead of guessing.

    Common error patterns to unlearn

    English speakers make three systematic errors. First, over-applying the showcase endings: writing einen guter Mann or mit einem guter Freund because guter "sounds like the ein-form". The -er/-es showcase exists only in nominative (and neuter accusative) — everywhere else after ein it's -en. Second, forgetting strong declension entirely: kalte Wasser instead of kaltes Wasser, because the weak -e default bleeds over. Third, dative plural without the noun's -n: den guten Männer instead of den guten Männern. Each error has one fix: run the three questions (table? gender? case?) before writing.

    Why weak -en is your best friend

    Step back and count: of the 48 cells across the three tables, -en fills 27 of them — more than half, including every dative, every genitive, and every plural after an article. That means a learner who defaults to -en after any article and only pauses for the showcase slots (weak -e × 5, mixed guter/gute/gutes) will be right the large majority of the time. This is not a hack; it's the statistical shape of the language. Build the -en reflex first with the quiz above, then sharpen the showcase exceptions.

    The ein-word family: mein, dein, kein

    Mixed declension isn't triggered by ein alone — the whole ein-word family behaves the same way: mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, kein. So the showcase endings apply to possessives too: mein guter Freund (not mein guten), deine gute Idee, kein gutes Wetter, unserem guten Lehrer (dative → -en). In the plural these words take -e endings of their own (meine, keine), and the adjective then uses plain -en: meine guten Freunde, keine guten Nachrichten. A frequent exam sentence: Das ist kein ___ Fehler — masculine accusative, mixed → keinen guten Fehler. Train possessives with the same three questions (table? gender? case?) and they'll stop feeling like a separate topic.

    Attributive vs. predicative — and adjectives turned into nouns

    Everything in this quiz concerns attributive adjectives — those standing directly before the noun. Predicative adjectives (after sein/werden/bleiben) never take endings: der Wein ist gut, never gut-er. Mixing the two is a classic beginner error (der Mann ist guter), so build the reflex: before the noun → decline; after sein → bare. One level deeper, German turns adjectives into nouns, and these nominalised adjectives decline exactly like attributive adjectives with the matching article: der Gute, ein Guter, die Schöne, etwas Gutes, viel Gutes. The capital letter plus the familiar ending is the giveaway. If you can decline der gute Mann, you can decline der Gute — same table, same slots.

    From quiz to real writing

    Quizzes build recognition; writing builds production. After each round here, take three sentences you answered and rewrite them in a different case: turn der gute Mann into dative (dem guten Mann) and genitive (des guten Mannes); turn ein gutes Kind into plural (gute Kinder / die guten Kinder). Say each version aloud. This transformation drill is what converts table knowledge into the automatic endings you need when speaking — because in conversation there is no time to consult a table, only to trust the reflex.

    Frequently asked questions

    Is it "ein guter Wein" or "ein guten Wein"?

    Ein guter Wein — masculine nominative, mixed declension: ein can't show the case, so the adjective takes -er. Accusative would be einen guten Wein.

    When do adjectives take the strong declension?

    With no article before the adjective: kaltes Wasser, deutsche Küche, gute Freunde. The adjective carries the full case ending itself.

    Why is it "der gute Mann" but "den guten Mann"?

    Both are weak declension. Nominative takes -e; masculine accusative takes -en. After der/die/das, only five slots take -e — everything else is -en.

    What is the most common adjective-ending mistake?

    Over-applying the showcase: einen guter Mann instead of einen guten Mann. The -er/-es endings appear only where the article can't show the case.

    How do adjective endings work with plural nouns?

    After any article: always -en (die guten Männer). With no article: -e in nominative/accusative (gute Freunde), -en in dative (guten Freunden).
