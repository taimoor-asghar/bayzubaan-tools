Why German etiquette feels like a rulebook

Newcomers often describe Germany as a country of rules — and they are half right. What looks like rigidity is usually Rücksicht (consideration) made visible: quiet hours, recycling systems, punctuality and greeting rituals are all mechanisms for strangers to coexist predictably in dense cities. The good news is that the rules are learnable, finite, and — once you know them — genuinely make life smoother. The bad news is that nobody will teach them to you; Germans assume everyone knows. This page collects the 24 unwritten rules that actually get newcomers scolded, judged or quietly frozen out, each with the reasoning behind it, plus a quiz that trains the judgment directly.

Pünktlichkeit, Sie, and the greeting economy

Pünktlichkeit (punctuality) is the most famous German virtue and the most misunderstood. It is not about loving clocks; it is about respecting other people's time as a scarce resource. Five minutes early is on time; arriving exactly on time for a social dinner is fine, but for anything professional, 'on time' already reads as slightly late. If you will be late, text — Verspätung ansagen (announcing your delay) is itself a rule, and failing to do it doubles the offence. The Sie/du distinction runs on the same logic of respect: Sie is the default with strangers, older people, and in professional life, and the switch to du is offered, never taken. Offering the du too early reads as presumptuous; withholding it after it has been offered reads as cold. When in doubt, Sie — nobody was ever insulted by being addressed too formally.

The greeting economy is small but mandatory: entering a small shop, a doctor's waiting room, or a lift with neighbours earns a 'Guten Tag' or at least a nod. Silence in these micro-spaces reads as hostile. At the table, nobody starts before 'Guten Appetit!' — usually given by the host or the eldest — and the toast requires 'Prost!' with eye contact to each person. The eye-contact rule is the one foreigners find strangest and Germans enforce most consistently; the folk belief (seven years of bad luck) is a joke, but the social signal is real: looking away while toasting reads as shifty.

Ruhe, Mülltrennung, and the Sunday laws

Ruhe (quiet) is the second pillar. The Nachtruhe (night quiet, roughly 22:00–06:00) and Mittagsruhe (midday quiet) are not suggestions — they are enforced by lease agreements and, in extreme cases, by the Ordnungsamt. Sundays are the big one: the Sonntagsruhe bans noisy work, which is why drilling a shelf at 10am on a Sunday can genuinely bring a neighbour to your door. Shops are closed (with rare exceptions), and the day belongs to rest, family walks and cake. Plan around it rather than fighting it: Saturday is the shopping day, Sunday is the recovery day, and within weeks the rhythm feels natural.

Mülltrennung (waste separation) is the third pillar and the one with the steepest learning curve: Gelber Sack for packaging, Papier for paper and cardboard, Biomüll for organic waste, Restmüll for the rest, plus glass sorted by colour at the neighbourhood containers (and never after 20:00 or on Sundays — Ruhe again). Getting it wrong is the fastest way to annoy a Hausverwaltung. The system works — Germany recycles at one of the highest rates in the world — and after the first month of checking the chart on the bin-room wall, it becomes automatic.

The taboos: birthdays, money, and the red man

Three German taboos surprise almost every newcomer. Never congratulate a birthday early. Wishing 'Happy birthday' before the day is considered bad luck, and Germans react with genuine discomfort — even rationalists. If you will miss the day, say nothing until it passes, then congratulate late with an apology. Never ask about salary — pay is private in a way that startles Americans and Brits alike; even colleagues who are friendly will freeze. Never cross against the red pedestrian light when children are watching. This one is almost comical to outsiders until a stranger actually scolds you — which happens. The red Ampelmännchen is treated as a moral lesson for children, and adults who jaywalk in front of them are seen as corrupting the youth. At 3am with empty streets, locals cross; at 3pm near a school, nobody does. Read the context.

Two smaller rules complete the picture. Validate your train ticket (entwerten) before boarding regional trains — inspectors show no mercy to the 'I didn't know' defence, and the fine starts around 60 euros. Don't call about work after 21:00 or on Sundays — Feierabend (quitting time) is a protected concept, and violating it marks you as someone who doesn't understand boundaries. And when a German says 'Fühl dich wie zu Hause' (make yourself at home), accept the warmth, not the literal invitation: ask before opening the fridge.

Frequently asked questions

Should I use du or Sie with strangers in Germany?

Sie — always, until it is explicitly waived. Use Sie with strangers, older people, and in professional settings. Offering du too early reads as presumptuous; the switch is offered by the senior or the host, never taken by the newcomer.

Why can't I wish happy birthday early in Germany?

It is considered bad luck, and Germans react with genuine discomfort — even self-described rationalists. If you'll miss the day, congratulate late with an apology instead of early.

Is jaywalking actually a problem in Germany?

Crossing against the red pedestrian light in front of children will get you scolded by strangers — the red Ampelmännchen is treated as a moral lesson for kids. At empty streets late at night locals cross; near schools in daytime, nobody does.

What are the Sunday quiet rules?

Sonntagsruhe bans noisy work on Sundays (drilling, mowing, loud music), shops stay closed, and glass-recycling containers are off-limits. Nachtruhe (roughly 22:00–06:00) applies daily. Violations can bring complaints to the Ordnungsamt.

How does tipping work in German restaurants?

Round up or add 5–10% for good service. The phrase is 'Stimmt so' (keep the change) when paying cash, or state the total you want to pay when paying by card. Service is included by law, so tipping is appreciation, not obligation.

Do I really have to sort my rubbish in Germany?

Yes — Gelber Sack (packaging), Papier, Biomüll, Restmüll, plus colour-sorted glass at neighbourhood containers. Getting it wrong is the fastest way to annoy your Hausverwaltung, and the system is a genuine point of national pride.
