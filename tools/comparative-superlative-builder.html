How comparatives and superlatives work in German

German adjectives have three degrees of comparison: the positive (schnell), the comparative (schneller — "faster"), and the superlative (am schnellsten — "fastest"). The good news is that the basic pattern is completely regular: add -er for the comparative and use am … -sten for the superlative. So klein becomes kleiner and am kleinsten. The irregularities you need to learn are the umlaut rule, a handful of e-drops, and six truly irregular adjectives — all covered in the builder above.

The umlaut rule

Many one-syllable adjectives containing a, o, or u add an umlaut in the comparative and superlative: alt → älter → am ältesten, jung → jünger, kalt → kälter, warm → wärmer, lang → länger, stark → stärker, groß → größer → am größten. Multi-syllable adjectives almost never take an umlaut (interessant → interessanter), and a few one-syllable adjectives resist it too (froh → froher, voll → voller). When in doubt, check the builder — it encodes the standard umlaut list.

E-dropping adjectives

Adjectives ending in -euer, -el, or -er drop the middle e in the comparative to keep pronunciation smooth: teuer → teurer (never "teuerer"), dunkel → dunkler, sauer → saurer. This is one of the most common spelling mistakes learners make — writing the e back in immediately marks you as a non-native.

The six irregulars

These must be memorised: gut → besser → am besten, gern → lieber → am liebsten, viel → mehr → am meisten, hoch → höher → am höchsten, nah → näher → am nächsten. Note the spelling quirks: hoch loses its c before the vowel, and nah changes its stem entirely. These are among the most frequent words in German, so drill them until they are automatic.

Comparative sentences: "als" vs "wie"

A classic learner error is confusing als and wie. The rule is simple: als goes with the comparative (größer als — "bigger than"), wie goes with the positive (so groß wie — "as big as"). The superlative takes am … -sten in predicative position (Dieser Zug ist am schnellsten) but declines normally as an attributive adjective: der schnellste Zug, die größte Stadt, das beste Hotel.

Attributive forms decline too

When the comparative or superlative stands before a noun, it takes normal adjective endings on top of the comparison ending: ein schnellerer Zug, die schnellste Verbindung, einem älteren Herrn. The comparison stem (schneller-, schnellst-) behaves like any other adjective stem — just apply the definite/indefinite/none endings you already know from the adjective declension drills.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. "Teuerer" instead of "teurer". The e-drop rule catches almost every learner once. Build the habit: any adjective ending in -euer, -el, or -er shortens in the comparative — teuer → teurer, dunkel → dunkler, sauer → saurer. 2. Missing the umlaut. Writing "alter" instead of "älter" is the classic tell of an English speaker. When you learn a new one-syllable adjective, learn its comparative form in the same breath. 3. "Als" with "wie". Größer wie is wrong; größer als is right. So groß wie is right. Say the pair out loud ten times and it sticks. 4. Double superlatives. "Am bestensten" does not exist — the superlative is already the maximum. 5. Forgetting to decline attributive forms. "Der schnellste Zug" needs that -e on schnellste; a bare "schnellst Zug" is a grammar error, not a shortcut.

Comparatives in everyday German

These patterns appear constantly in real life, which makes them high-value to automate. At the doctor's: "Es tut mehr weh" (it hurts more), "Die Schmerzen sind schlimmer geworden" (the pain got worse). Shopping: "Haben Sie etwas Billigeres?" (do you have something cheaper?), "Das ist das beste Angebot" (that's the best offer). At work: "Könnten Sie das bitte etwas langsamer erklären?" (could you explain that a bit more slowly?). With friends: "Ich hätte lieber den roten" (I'd prefer the red one), "Das war der schönste Abend seit langem" (that was the nicest evening in a long time). Notice how naturally lieber expresses preference — Germans reach for it instead of the clunky "ich bevorzuge". Also note mehr and weniger do the heavy lifting for quantities: "mehr Zeit", "weniger Stress". Practise by narrating your day in comparisons: your coffee was stärker than yesterday's, the queue was länger, and the weather is definitely schöner.

Frequently asked questions

How do you form the comparative in German?

Add -er to the adjective: schnell → schneller. One-syllable adjectives with a, o, or u usually add an umlaut: alt → älter, groß → größer. Adjectives in -euer, -el, -er drop the e: teuer → teurer.

How do you form the superlative in German?

In predicative position use am + -sten: am schnellsten, am größten. Before a noun, use the declined form: der schnellste Zug, die größte Stadt. Irregulars: am besten, am liebsten, am meisten, am höchsten, am nächsten.

Which German adjectives are irregular in comparison?

Six must be memorised: gut → besser → am besten, gern → lieber → am liebsten, viel → mehr → am meisten, hoch → höher → am höchsten, nah → näher → am nächsten. The e-droppers (teuer → teurer, dunkel → dunkler) follow a pattern rather than being fully irregular.

When do you use 'als' and when 'wie' in comparisons?

Use 'als' with the comparative (größer als — bigger than) and 'wie' with the positive (so groß wie — as big as). Mixing these up is the most common comparison error among English speakers.

Do comparative adjectives need umlauts?

One-syllable adjectives with a, o, or u usually do: alt → älter, jung → jünger, kalt → kälter. Longer adjectives do not (interessanter). A few one-syllable adjectives also skip it (froher, voller).
