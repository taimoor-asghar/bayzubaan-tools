The myth: Germans hate small talk

Ask the internet and you will hear that Germans despise small talk — that they find it shallow, pointless, and faintly suspicious. The reality is more interesting: Germans do small talk constantly, they just do it differently. What counts as acceptable small talk in Germany is narrower, more situational, and more honest than the Anglo version. Nobody asks 'How are you?' expecting 'Fine!' as a reflex; the famous German opener Na, wie geht's? actually invites a real answer, and giving one ('Ach, ganz gut, aber die Arbeit ist stressig') is not oversharing — it is the point. The 40 phrases on this page are the working vocabulary of German everyday sociability: greetings that open doors, weather talk that fills lifts, work questions that start dinners, and goodbyes that end them warmly.

The golden rule of German small talk is situational honesty. English small talk often runs on cheerful fiction ('Great! You?'); German small talk runs on shared observation. That is why the weather dominates: it is true, it is shared, and everyone can verify it. 'Schönes Wetter heute, oder?' in a lift is not a failure of imagination — it is a low-risk bid for connection that the other person can accept ('Ja, endlich mal Sonne!') or deflect with a smile. The phrases in the Wetter tab are the highest-frequency small talk in the entire language; master them first and you will never stand in awkward silence again.

The five zones of Alltag conversation

Begrüßung & Einstieg — German openers are direct. 'Na, wie geht's?' and 'Na, alles klar?' are the casual defaults among people who know each other; 'Lange nicht gesehen!' reactivates dormant contacts; 'Was gibt's Neues?' is the standard catch-up prompt. Note the follow-up culture: Germans ask the question back ('und selbst?', 'und deins?'), and failing to return the question reads as self-absorbed. The mini-dialogues on this page model exactly this two-step rhythm — question, answer, return — because the return is where learners most often drop the ball.

Arbeit & Studium — 'Was machst du beruflich?' is the standard get-to-know-you question, asked earlier in Germany than in many cultures and without the status anxiety it can carry elsewhere; it is genuine interest in what fills your days. 'Wie läuft's auf der Arbeit?' works as a check-in with colleagues. Two idioms punch above their weight: 'Ich habe gerade viel um die Ohren' (I've got a lot on — literally 'a lot around the ears') is the graceful decline, and 'Ich drücke dir die Daumen!' (I'll press my thumbs for you — the German 'fingers crossed') is the warm send-off before exams and interviews. Using the Daumen phrase marks you as an insider instantly.

Wochenende & Freizeit — weekend plans are the Friday currency of German offices: 'Was hast du am Wochenende vor?' on Friday, 'Wie war dein Wochenende?' on Monday. These are not empty rituals; Germans genuinely plan weekends and genuinely want to hear about yours. 'Lust auf einen Kaffee?' is the lowest-commitment invitation in the language — coffee is the default social medium — and 'Sollen wir uns mal treffen?' is how acquaintances become friends. A useful calibration: 'Das klingt gut!' accepts warmly, 'Ich bin total k.o.' declines without offence (everyone respects exhaustion).

Verabschiedung — Germans say goodbye thoroughly, sometimes at greater length than the conversation itself. 'Mach's gut!' (take care), 'Man sieht sich!' (see you around), 'Komm gut nach Hause!' (get home safe) and 'Melde dich mal!' (keep in touch) layer on top of each other, and each carries a slightly different warmth. 'Schönes Wochenende!' / 'Danke, gleichfalls!' is the Friday office liturgy — learn the 'gleichfalls' (likewise) response and you will sound native by week two.

Topics: the safe, the risky, and the German third category

Safe small-talk topics in Germany: the weather, travel ('Warst du schon mal in Berlin?'), food and restaurants, weekend plans, football (during tournaments, universally), and — genuinely — work. Risky topics: salary (never — see the etiquette quiz), politics with strangers, religion, and personal health details. Then there is the German third category: topics that are normal in Germany but feel intimate to outsiders, like direct questions about rent prices, detailed holiday itineraries, or frank opinions about your cooking. 'Was zahlst du für die Wohnung?' (what do you pay for the flat?) is asked between acquaintances with zero embarrassment. Don't flinch; it is curiosity, not intrusion.

One more calibration: Germans complain — cheerfully, socially, constantly. 'So ein mieses Wetter!', 'Die Prüfung war ganz schön schwer', 'Ich bin total k.o.' — shared grumbling is bonding, not negativity. The correct response is agreement plus your own contribution ('Fand ich auch'), not toxic positivity. A learner who can grumble in German has arrived.

Frequently asked questions

Do Germans really hate small talk?

Not exactly — they do it differently. German small talk runs on shared observation (especially the weather) rather than cheerful fiction, and 'Na, wie geht's?' invites a real answer. What Germans dislike is empty ritual, not conversation itself.

What is the most common German small talk topic?

The weather, by a wide margin — it is true, shared, and verifiable. 'Schönes Wetter heute, oder?' is the highest-frequency opener in lifts, shops and offices. Weekend plans ('Was hast du am Wochenende vor?') come second.

What does 'Na, wie geht's?' literally mean and how do I answer?

Literally 'Well, how's it going?' — casual. Answer honestly and briefly ('Ach, ganz gut, und selbst?'), then return the question. Failing to ask back reads as self-absorbed in German conversation culture.

What topics should I avoid in German small talk?

Salary (strictly taboo), politics and religion with strangers, and personal health details. Safe: weather, travel, food, weekend plans, football, and work ('Was machst du beruflich?' is asked early and warmly).

What is the German equivalent of 'fingers crossed'?

'Ich drücke dir die Daumen!' — literally 'I'll press my thumbs for you'. Germans press thumbs, not cross fingers. Using it before someone's exam or interview marks you as an insider instantly.

How do Germans say goodbye?

Thoroughly: 'Mach's gut!' (take care), 'Man sieht sich!' (see you around), 'Komm gut nach Hause!' (get home safe), 'Melde dich mal!' (keep in touch). On Fridays: 'Schönes Wochenende!' — answer 'Danke, gleichfalls!'
