Why adjectives change at all

German adjectives sit in front of nouns and advertise the noun's gender, number and case — but only when the article doesn't already do the job. That's the entire logic of the system: the case information must appear exactly once in the noun phrase. If the article shows it (der, dem, einem), the adjective relaxes with a simple -en. If the article is missing or can't show it (ein, no article at all), the adjective steps up and carries the full ending itself. Once you internalise this "one signal per phrase" principle, the three declension tables stop being random lists and become predictable.

Weak declension: after der/die/das

After a definite article (also dieser, jeder, jener, welcher, mancher in the singular), the adjective is on easy street. The ending is -en everywhere except five -e slots: masculine nominative (der gute Mann), feminine nominative and accusative (die gute Frau), neuter nominative and accusative (das gute Kind). Everything else — all datives, all genitives, all plurals, masculine and neuter accusative — takes -en: den guten Mann, dem guten Mann, des guten Mannes, die guten Männer, den guten Männern. This table alone covers the majority of adjective phrases you'll ever write, and it's worth memorising as your default: when in doubt after der/die/das, guess -en.

Mixed declension: after ein/eine

The indefinite article ein is ambiguous — ein Mann could be nominative masculine or nominative/accusative neuter — so the adjective helps out, but only where needed. Three showcase endings do the work: masculine nominative -er (ein guter Mann), neuter nominative and accusative -es (ein gutes Kind), feminine nominative and accusative -e (eine gute Frau). Everywhere else the article already carries the case, so the adjective falls back to -en: einen guten Mann, einem guten Mann, eines guten Mannes, einer guten Frau, keine guten Ideen. Note the plural: with keine/meine/alle it's always -en in the plural.

Strong declension: no article

With no article, the adjective takes the strong endings — essentially the same endings as the der-words: masculine nominative -er (guter Mann), neuter nominative/accusative -es (gutes Kind), dative masculine/neuter -em (gutem Mann), dative feminine -er (guter Frau), genitive feminine/plural -er (guter Frau, guter Freunde), and -en for the rest. You meet strong declension in phrases like kaltes Wasser, deutsche Küche, frisches Brot and in the plural without articles: gute Freunde (nom./acc.), guten Freunden (dat. — with the -n on the noun), guter Freunde (gen.).

The five endings you actually need

Here's the practical secret: the whole system uses only five endings — -e, -er, -en, -em, -es. And -en does the overwhelming majority of the work: it's the default after definite and indefinite articles outside the showcase slots. So the learning strategy is inverted: don't memorise 48 cells; memorise the exceptions to -en. After der/die/das: -e in the five slots (masc. nom.; fem. nom./acc.; neut. nom./acc.). After ein: -er (masc. nom.), -es (neut. nom./acc.), -e (fem. nom./acc.). With no article: use the strong row above. Three small lists beat one big table.

Adjectives that never change

Two situations need no ending at all. Predicate adjectives — after sein, werden, bleiben — are always bare: der Mann ist gut, das Wetter wird schön, die Suppe bleibt kalt. And a handful of borrowed colour adjectives (rosa, lila, orange) traditionally stay uninflected attributively: das rosa Kleid, ein lila Hut (inflected rosane, lilane forms exist in speech but remain informal). Adverbs made from adjectives are also bare: er spricht gut (adverb) vs. ein guter Sprecher (adjective).

How to practise

Use the calculator above as a checker, not a crutch: write ten noun phrases by hand (der ___ Mann, einem ___ Kind, kalte ___), fill in the endings from memory, then verify each with the tool and read the rule it gives you. Drill the weak table first — it's the most frequent — then the mixed showcase endings, then strong. Within a week of five-minute sessions, the -en default will be automatic and you'll only pause for the showcase slots, which is exactly where native-speed German lives.

Frequently asked questions

What is the adjective ending for masculine accusative with the definite article?

-en: den guten Mann. After der/die/das, almost every ending is -en — the only -e exceptions are masculine nominative, feminine nominative/accusative, and neuter nominative/accusative.

What is the difference between weak, mixed and strong declension?

Weak (der/die/das): mostly -en. Mixed (ein/eine): -en except ein guter (-er), eine gute (-e), ein gutes (-es). Strong (no article): the adjective carries the full case ending.

Which adjectives don't take endings?

Predicate adjectives after sein/werden/bleiben (der Mann ist gut), and borrowed colours like rosa, lila, orange in attributive position.

How do I remember German adjective endings?

Default to -en after any article; memorise only the exceptions: five -e slots after der/die/das, and the three showcase endings after ein (guter, gute, gutes).

Do adjective endings change in the plural?

After an article, plural adjectives are always -en (die guten Männer). With no article: -e in nominative/accusative (gute Freunde), -en in dative (guten Freunden).
