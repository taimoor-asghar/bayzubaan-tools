What the accusative case actually is

The accusative is the German case for the direct object — the person or thing that directly receives the action of the verb. You find it by asking Wen oder was? ("Whom or what?"). In the sentence Ich sehe den Mann ("I see the man"), the question Wen sehe ich? ("Whom do I see?") gives den Mann: the man is the direct object, so he stands in the accusative.

English barely shows case anymore, which is why the accusative feels foreign at first. The good news is that the accusative only changes one thing about the definite articles — and once you see the table, drills like the one below become almost mechanical.

The accusative article table (compared with nominative)

Here is the complete table of definite articles in the nominative (subject) and accusative (direct object) cases:

Gender / numberNominativeAccusative
Masculinederden (changes!)
Femininediedie (unchanged)
Neuterdasdas (unchanged)
Pluraldiedie (unchanged)

This gives the classic learner's mantra: der → den. Everything else stays put. So Ich sehe den Mann but Ich sehe die Frau, Ich sehe das Kind, and Ich sehe die Kinder — only the masculine article visibly shifts.

The masculine-only-change insight: When you are unsure which article fits a blank, first ask whether the noun is masculine. If it is, the answer is den. If it is feminine, neuter, or plural, the article is simply the same as in the nominative — die, das, or die. This one rule answers most accusative questions in everyday German.

Accusative prepositions

Certain German prepositions always force the accusative, whatever comes after them. The five core ones are durch (through), für (for), gegen (against), ohne (without), and um (around / at, for times). Two more belong to the family: bis (until, up to) and entlang (along — used after the noun). Examples: für den Hund ("for the dog"), ohne die Brille ("without the glasses"), um das Haus ("around the house"), durch den Park ("through the park"), gegen den Wind ("against the wind").

Note that the prepositions in this drill's sentences are not the point — the quiz trains verb + direct-object accusatives, which are far more common in daily speech. But recognising durch, für, gegen, ohne, um on sight is a reliable exam skill, and every one of these pairs naturally with the den/die/das choice you are practising.

Common verbs that take the accusative

A large family of everyday verbs governs a direct object in the accusative. The most frequent are: sehen (to see), haben (to have), kaufen (to buy), lesen (to read), trinken (to drink), essen (to eat), brauchen (to need), kennen (to know, be acquainted with), besuchen (to visit), lieben and mögen (to love / to like), öffnen (to open), schreiben (to write), fragen (to ask), and suchen (to look for).

All fifteen drill sentences use verbs from this list: Ich trinke den Kaffee, Sie schreibt den Brief, Wir brauchen die Hilfe. When you meet a new verb, learn it together with its case — "kaufen + Akkusativ" as one chunk — and you will rarely go wrong.

Common mistakes to avoid

1. Forgetting der → den. By far the most frequent error: learners write Ich sehe der Mann because the nominative form is stuck in their head. Whenever the masculine noun is the direct object, the article must become den.

2. Over-correcting feminine and neuter nouns. The opposite error is "fixing" articles that were already right: Ich sehe die Frau needs no change, and Er kauft das Auto is already correct. Only masculine changes.

3. Treating plurals as a special case. The plural article is die in both nominative and accusative, so Er sieht die Kinder and Wir sehen die Berge need no article change at all — just recognise the plural and keep die.

4. Confusing accusative with dative prepositions. Some prepositions (like mit or von) always take the dative. Do not apply the accusative table to them. This trainer sticks to pure accusative contexts so the pattern can settle before you add the dative layer.

A quick self-check for every sentence

Before you commit to an answer, run this three-second routine. First, name the verb and ask Wen oder was? — that locates the direct object and confirms the accusative is even involved. Second, identify the noun's gender and number: is it masculine, feminine, neuter, or plural? Use a dictionary or your vocabulary list for any noun you are unsure about; guessing gender is where most points are lost. Third, apply the table: masculine gives den, everything else keeps its nominative form (die, das, or plural die). Try it on Sie schreibt ___ Brief: the verb schreiben takes an accusative object (Wen oder was schreibt sie? — den Brief), Brief is masculine (der Brief), and masculine accusative is den. With a little repetition this sequence becomes automatic, and automatic is exactly what you need when you start speaking.
