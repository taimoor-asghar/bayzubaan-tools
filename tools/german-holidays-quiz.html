Germany's holiday system: simple on paper, complicated in practice

Germany has nine public holidays observed nationwide: Neujahr (1 January), Karfreitag, Ostermontag, Tag der Arbeit (1 May), Christi Himmelfahrt, Pfingstmontag, Tag der Deutschen Einheit (3 October), and the two Christmas days (25/26 December). Everything else is Ländersache — a matter for the sixteen federal states. Heilige Drei Könige (6 January) is a holiday in Bavaria, Baden-Württemberg and Saxony-Anhalt but a normal workday in Berlin. Allerheiligen (1 November) splits the country along old confessional lines: holiday in the Catholic south and west, workday in the Protestant north and east. Fronleichnam follows the same Catholic map, and Buß- und Bettag survives as a public holiday only in Saxony. If you move from Munich to Hamburg, your holiday calendar literally changes — a fact that surprises newcomers every year and one this quiz drills deliberately.

Tag der Deutschen Einheit deserves special attention because it is the only holiday established by federal law rather than state law — 3 October, marking reunification in 1990. The official celebration rotates between state capitals each year. Note what Germany does not have: unlike many countries, there is no automatic Monday off when a holiday falls on a weekend. A 3 October on a Sunday simply means one fewer day off that year, and Germans track this with the mild resentment of people who have internalised the loss.

The movable feasts: why Easter moves everything

Half the spring calendar is beweglich — movable — because it is computed from Easter Sunday, which falls on the first Sunday after the first full moon of spring (anywhere from 22 March to 25 April). From that one date the whole chain hangs: Weiberfastnacht and Rosenmontag (carnival's climax, the Thursday and Monday before Ash Wednesday), Aschermittwoch (46 days before Easter), Ostermontag, Christi Himmelfahrt (39 days after Easter Sunday), Pfingstsonntag and Pfingstmontag (seven weeks after Easter), and Fronleichnam (60 days after Easter). Learn the offsets and you can reconstruct any year's calendar from Easter alone — a party trick that also happens to be the logic the quiz tests. The Advent season is movable too: the first Advent is the fourth Sunday before Christmas, which is why Christmas markets open on different dates each year and why the quiz lists it as beweglich.

Two November feasts catch learners out because they sound similar and sit close together: Volkstrauertag (the second Sunday before the first Advent — the national day of mourning) and Totensonntag (the Sunday immediately before the first Advent — the Protestant Sunday of the Dead). Both are stille Tage (quiet days): in several states, public dancing and loud entertainment are legally restricted. Buß- und Bettag sits between them on the Wednesday of that week. Three sombre November days in a row, each with its own name — the quiz separates them because exams and integration tests love exactly this cluster.

Traditions that are not holidays (but matter more socially)

Some of Germany's most important dates are not public holidays at all, and missing them socially costs more than missing a day off. Nikolaus (6 December): children polish their boots and leave them out overnight; good children find treats, and tradition supplies Knecht Ruprecht for the others. Heiligabend (24 December): this — not the 25th — is the German Christmas: the Bescherung (gift-giving) happens on Christmas Eve evening around the Tannenbaum, and shops close early. Foreigners who schedule Christmas dinner for the 25th and expect Germans free on the 24th have the rhythm backwards. Silvester (31 December): private fireworks are the tradition (with annual debates about bans), plus Wachsgießen (fortune-telling with molten wax) and the 1963 British comedy sketch Dinner for One, broadcast every year without fail. Sankt Martin (11 November): lantern processions of children singing through the dark streets — and, at 11:11 on 11.11, the official opening of carnival season.

Karneval itself — called Fasching in Bavaria and Austria, Fastnacht in the southwest — is the fifth season in the Rhineland, running from 11 November to Ash Wednesday with its climax in the week before Lent: Weiberfastnacht (Thursday), Rosenmontag (Monday, the great parades of Cologne, Düsseldorf and Mainz), and the whole country in costume. Tanz in den Mai (30 April) raises the Maibaum and lights Maifeuer bonfires. Erntedankfest (first Sunday in October) decorates church altars with harvest produce. Oktoberfest — despite the name — runs mostly in September, ending on the first Sunday in October; Tracht is the dress code and the quiz entry notes the dates precisely because the name misleads.

Frequently asked questions

How many public holidays does Germany have?

Nine are observed nationwide (Neujahr, Karfreitag, Ostermontag, Tag der Arbeit, Christi Himmelfahrt, Pfingstmontag, Tag der Deutschen Einheit, 25. and 26. Dezember). The states add their own: Bavaria has the most (13), and only Saxony still keeps Buß- und Bettag. Tag der Deutschen Einheit is the only holiday set by federal law.

Why do German holidays move every year?

The spring feasts hang off Easter Sunday (first Sunday after the first full moon of spring): Rosenmontag, Aschermittwoch, Ostermontag, Christi Himmelfahrt (+39 days), Pfingstmontag (+7 weeks) and Fronleichnam (+60 days) all shift with it. Advent Sundays are computed backwards from Christmas, so they move too.

Is Christmas Eve or Christmas Day the main celebration in Germany?

Christmas Eve — Heiligabend, 24 December. The Bescherung (gift-giving) happens on Christmas Eve evening around the tree; the 25th and 26th are the quiet family feast days. Shops close early on the 24th.

What is the difference between Volkstrauertag and Totensonntag?

Both are sombre November Sundays just before Advent: Volkstrauertag (two Sundays before the first Advent) is the national day of mourning for victims of war and tyranny; Totensonntag (the Sunday right before the first Advent) is the Protestant day of remembrance for the dead. Both are 'stille Tage' with entertainment restrictions in several states.

When is carnival in Germany?

The season opens 11.11. at 11:11 and climaxes in the week before Ash Wednesday: Weiberfastnacht (Thursday), Rosenmontag (Monday, the big parades), then Aschermittwoch ends it. Strongholds are the Rhineland (Karneval), Bavaria (Fasching) and the southwest (Fastnacht).

Do Germans get a day off when a holiday falls on a weekend?

No — unlike many countries, Germany has no 'observed Monday' rule. A holiday on a Saturday or Sunday is simply lost, which is why Germans watch the weekday distribution of each year's calendar closely.
