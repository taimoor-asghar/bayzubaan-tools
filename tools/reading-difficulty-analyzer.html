Why reading level matters more than you think

Most German learners read at the wrong level. Too easy, and you coast without learning; too hard, and every sentence is a dictionary battle that kills motivation within a week. Research on extensive reading is unambiguous: the sweet spot is material where you already understand 95–98% of the words — the famous "i+1" zone, where the unknown 2–5% is guessable from context and therefore learnable. The problem is that "B1 reader" labels on book covers are publisher marketing, not measurement. This analyser gives you an actual measurement: paste any text — a news article, a chapter, your own writing — and get a CEFR estimate grounded in seven measurable linguistic features. Use it before you commit to a 300-page novel.

The 7 features the analyser measures

1. Average sentence length. The single strongest readability signal in German. A1 texts average under 8 words per sentence; C1 academic prose pushes past 20, because subordinate clauses nest inside each other. If your sentences average 16+ words, you're reading B2+ material whether the cover says so or not. 2. Average word length. German compounds inflate this fast: Urlaubserlebnisse is one "word" but three concepts. Beginner texts stay under 4.6 characters per word; advanced prose exceeds 6.5. 3. Long-word ratio. The share of words over 6 characters. Below 16% is beginner-friendly; above 42% is dense specialist prose. 4. Subordination density. Counts subordinating conjunctions (weil, dass, obwohl, wenn, sodass, weshalb) and relative pronouns per 100 words. Zero means paratactic beginner style; 8+ per 100 words means heavily nested B2/C1 syntax. 5. Subjunctive density. würde, hätte, wäre, könnte — hypothetical and reported-speech forms that appear from B1 upward and saturate C1. 6. Advanced connectors. dennoch, jedoch, insofern, zudem, beispielsweise, hinsichtlich — the discourse glue of argumentative writing, rare below B2. 7. Passive density. wird/wurde + participle — formal written German loves the passive; its absence marks spoken-like beginner text. Each feature scores 0–4 (subjunctive/advanced connectors 0–3, passive 0–2); the total out of 28 maps to a band.

How the bands map to CEFR

0–6: A1. Short sentences, everyday vocabulary, no subordination. Think children's books and the first graded readers — perfect if you're in your first months. 7–11: A2. Simple past narration appears (bin … gegangen), plus the first subordinate clauses. Nachrichten in einfacher Sprache lives here. 12–16: B1. Relative clauses, opinions, first hypotheticals (würde). Simple newspaper articles, B1 exam reading sections. 17–20: B2. Argumentation, passive voice, advanced connectors. Standard Tagesschau articles and B2 exam texts. 21–28: C1+. Dense, nominal, subjunctive-heavy prose — editorials, Fachtexte, literature. If your own writing scores here, your written German is genuinely advanced.

Using it on your own writing

The analyser is a mirror, not just a ruler. Paste something you wrote in German and read the metric bars, not just the band. Sentence length stuck at 9 words after a year of study? You're writing like a textbook dialogue — deliberately practise weil/dass/obwohl clauses. Zero subjunctive at B2? Start peppering würde into opinions. Long-word ratio under 20% at B1? Your vocabulary is still survival-level; the top-2000 quiz is the fix. The single most useful diagnostic is the gap between your reading band and your writing band: paste a news article (B2), then paste your summary of it. If your summary scores A2, the gap names your exact homework — usually subordination and connectors, the two features that separate "can understand" from "can produce."

Picking reading material with the score

Rule of thumb: read one band below your placement-test level for pleasure, at your level for study. If you placed B1, novels labelled B1 will feel like work (they're written at the top of the band); B1-graded readers at score 12–13 are the study material, while A2+ texts (score 9–11) are the fluency builders you actually finish. Paste the first page of any book before buying — publishers' CEFR labels are aspirational. Three quick checks beat one long one: the opening page, a middle page, and a dialogue-heavy page, because dialogue always scores lower than narration. If all three land in your i+1 zone, buy the book.

What the analyser can't see (honest limits)

It measures form, not meaning. A philosophical A2-length sentence can be harder than a long B1 one; idioms, cultural references, and irony are invisible to it. Poetry and song lyrics break the sentence-length metric entirely. Genre matters: transcribed speech (interviews, podcasts) scores lower than its true difficulty because spoken German uses short main clauses — don't conclude a podcast is "easy" from a B1 score; the listening challenge is speed and reduction, not syntax (check the listening readiness quiz for that). Treat the band as a strong prior, not a verdict — then confirm with the reading-comprehension sections of real placement tests.

Your reading ladder: a practical plan

A1 → A2: graded readers at score 4–8, one per week; look up nothing, guess from context, keep a 5-words-per-day notebook. A2 → B1: Nachrichten in einfacher Sprache daily (score ~9), plus one B1 reader per fortnight; start the weil/dass clause habit in a reading journal. B1 → B2: real news (Tagesschau app, score 15–18) with the analyser open — paste anything that felt hard and find which metric spiked; that's your lesson. B2 → C1: opinion pieces and essays (score 19–22); shadow the connectors — copy three dennoch/insofern/zudem sentences per article into your own writing. Re-analyse your own writing monthly: a rising score is the most honest proof your German is actually advancing, more honest than any streak counter.

Frequently asked questions

How accurate is the reading difficulty estimate?

It is a heuristic based on 7 measurable features (sentence length, word length, subordination, subjunctive, connectors, passive) calibrated against reference texts at each CEFR level. It measures linguistic form reliably; it cannot judge idioms, irony, or cultural difficulty. Use it to choose reading material, then confirm your level with a placement test.

What text length works best?

At least 3–4 sentences (~15+ words); 100–300 words gives the most stable estimate. Very short texts produce noisy scores because one long sentence skews the average.

Can I analyse my own German writing?

Yes — that is one of the best uses. Paste your text and compare its band with your placement-test level. A gap (e.g. you read B2 but write A2) pinpoints exactly what to practise: usually subordination and connectors.

Why does dialogue score lower than narration?

Spoken-style German uses short main clauses, so the sentence-length and subordination metrics drop. A B1-scored podcast transcript can still be hard because of speed and reduced forms — try the listening readiness quiz for the audio side.

What score should my reading material have?

Read one band below your level for pleasure (fluency) and at your level for study (growth). Aim for texts where you know 95–98% of the words — the i+1 zone where the rest is guessable from context.

Does it work for Swiss or Austrian texts?

Yes for standard written German in any country — the metrics are grammatical, not lexical. Dialect-heavy dialogue (Bavarian, Swiss German) may score oddly; standard Hochdeutsch is what it is calibrated on.
