Travel German: the 45 phrases that actually matter

Travel phrasebooks fail in a predictable way: they give you 500 phrases you will never say and skip the 45 you will say ten times a day. This tool is the other approach — 45 phrases grouped by the five situations that structure every trip to a German-speaking country: the hotel, the restaurant, asking directions, transport, and emergencies. Each phrase comes with a usage note because a phrase without its context is a trap: Stimmt so is the right thing to say when paying cash in a restaurant and a bizarre thing to say at a hotel reception desk.

The design principle is production, not recognition. You do not need to understand every word a waiter says — you need to produce the right sentence at the right moment, under pressure, possibly tired and hungry. That is why the quiz runs English→German only: it trains the hard direction, the one the trip actually demands. Read the English situation, pick the German phrase a local would use, and read it aloud when you answer. Silent clicking builds recognition; speaking builds travel German.

Hotels: check-in to check-out

German hotels are formal but straightforward. At check-in: Ich habe ein Zimmer auf den Namen Schmidt reserviert (add your name). Ask Ist das Frühstück inbegriffen? — it usually is, and German hotel breakfasts are worth planning your morning around. Um wie viel Uhr ist das Frühstück? matters because it ends sharply, often at 10. Check-out (auschecken) is typically 11 Uhr; Wann muss man auschecken? asked on arrival prevents the awkward knock on the door.

If something is wrong with the room, say so directly: Das Zimmer ist zu laut, Könnte ich bitte ein anderes Zimmer bekommen? Germans do not consider this rude — directness about a service problem is normal and expected. What is rude is suffering in silence and then leaving a bad review. And note WLAN (pronounced "veh-lahn"): it is the German word for Wi-Fi, from wireless local area network, and asking Gibt es WLAN im Zimmer? marks you as someone who has been to Germany before.

Restaurants: ordering like a local

The restaurant script: Einen Tisch für zwei, bitte → Die Speisekarte, bitte → Ich hätte gern das Schnitzel → Das hat sehr gut geschmeckt → Die Rechnung, bitte. Note Ich hätte gern — the polite ordering formula, literally "I would gladly have". Ich will is for children and demands; adults use hätte gern or möchte. In Biergärten and busy cafés, tables are shared: Ist hier noch frei? is the essential phrase, asked before sitting at a table with strangers.

Two money rules. First: the bill never comes automatically in Germany — Die Rechnung, bitte is mandatory, and waiting for it to appear is waiting forever. Second: tipping. There is no percentage rule; you round up and say Stimmt so ("keep the change") when paying cash, or state the total you want to pay when paying by card (45 Euro, bitte on a 41.50 bill). Five to ten percent, rounded, handed directly to the server — never left on the table.

Directions and transport: moving through the country

Asking directions: Wie komme ich zum Stadtzentrum?, Wo ist der Bahnhof?, Ist es weit von hier? What you will hear back: geradeaus (straight on), links/rechts abbiegen (turn left/right), an der Ampel (at the traffic lights), gleich um die Ecke (just around the corner). If words fail, Können Sie mir das auf dem Stadtplan zeigen? — pointing at a map is a universal language, and Germans are genuinely helpful to lost visitors. Ich habe mich verlaufen said with a smile gets you rescued.

Transport runs on precision. At the station: Von welchem Gleis fährt der Zug ab? — always confirm the platform, because German stations have many. Muss ich umsteigen? decides whether your trip is relaxing or stressful. Ist das der Zug nach München? asked on the platform has saved countless travellers from expensive wrong-train rides. For city transport, Wie viel kostet eine Tageskarte? — the day ticket almost always beats single tickets. And in a taxi, Können Sie mich hier rauslassen? plus Wie lange dauert die Fahrt? keeps you oriented and honestly priced.

Emergencies: the nine you hope never to use

Learn these cold before you travel. Hilfe! Rufen Sie einen Krankenwagen! — and the number is 112, Europe-wide, not 911. Ich brauche einen Arzt. Wo ist das nächste Krankenhaus? For the police report after a theft: Mein Handy wurde gestohlen, Ich habe meine Papiere verloren. And the two social phrases that unlock help everywhere: Ich spreche nur wenig Deutsch (disarms the situation, invites English) and Können Sie bitte langsamer sprechen? (perfectly polite, use it freely). Ich verstehe nicht is always better than nodding along to something important.

The deeper emergency strategy: Was bedeutet das? — point at the sign, the form, the machine, and ask. Germans will not just translate; they will walk you through it. The phrase turns every local into a guide, and it is the single most useful sentence in this entire tool.

How to drill before the trip

One week before departure, do this daily: browse one category, read every phrase aloud twice (pronunciation matters — Ich hätte gern mumbled is useless), then run the 15-round quiz. The quiz draws distractors from the same situation, so Die Rechnung, bitte competes against Die Speisekarte, bitte — the exact confusion pair that strikes at the table. When you score 15/15 twice, do the final drill: pick five situations (check-in, ordering, asking directions, buying a ticket, an emergency) and deliver the phrases aloud as a monologue, no reading. That is the trip, rehearsed.

On the trip itself, keep this page on your phone. The browse view is your cheat sheet — category tabs get you to the phrase in seconds, faster than any phrasebook app. And remember the golden rule of travel German: attempt it first. Ich spreche nur wenig Deutsch, aber… followed by your best attempt earns you goodwill that fluent English never will.

Frequently asked questions

What is the emergency number in Germany?

112 — Europe-wide, for ambulance, fire, and police. Not 911. The phrase to know: Hilfe! Rufen Sie einen Krankenwagen!

Do I need to ask for the bill in German restaurants?

Yes — Die Rechnung, bitte. The bill never comes automatically in Germany; waiting for it is waiting forever.

How do I tip in Germany?

Round up 5–10% and say Stimmt so (keep the change) when paying cash, or state the total when paying by card. Never leave the tip on the table — hand it to the server.

What does 'Ist hier noch frei?' mean?

Is this seat free? Essential in Biergärten and cafés where tables are shared — always ask before sitting with strangers.

What is WLAN in German?

Wi-Fi. WLAN (pronounced 'veh-lahn') is the standard German word — asking Gibt es WLAN im Zimmer? is completely normal.

Should I attempt German or just speak English when travelling?

Attempt German first. Ich spreche nur wenig Deutsch, aber… followed by your best effort earns goodwill that fluent English never will — and most Germans will happily switch to English after.
