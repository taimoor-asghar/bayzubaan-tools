Numbers are the fastest, least forgiving part of spoken German. A cashier says "vier Euro fünfundachtzig" and you have about one second to decode it — no context, no second chance, and the German habit of saying the units before the tens ("fünfundachtzig", literally "five-and-eighty") means your English-trained ear has to run the number backwards. Prices, phone numbers, platform announcements, years: numbers are everywhere in daily life, and they are the single most common cause of "I understood everything except the important part." This drill trains exactly that: hear a number, recognise it instantly.

Why German numbers are hard to hear

Four things conspire against learners. 1. The inversion: 47 is "siebenundvierzig" (seven-and-forty), 21 is "einundzwanzig". English speakers hear "sieben..." and reach for 7x, then have to hold it while "vierzig" arrives — the mental buffer overflows at speed. 2. The traps: sechzehn (16) drops the s of sechs, siebzig (70) drops the en of sieben, dreißig (30) has ß where vierzig has z. Mishear one phoneme and 60 becomes 70. 3. The length: 1,985 is "eintausendneunhundertfünfundachtzig" — nine syllables where English uses six ("nineteen eighty-five"). Long German number words blur together at natural speed. 4. The comma: German prices use a comma where English uses a point (3,99 € is "drei Euro neunundneunzig"), and years are said in full ("eintausendneunhundertfünfundachtzig"), never paired like "nineteen eighty-five". Each of these gets its own round type in the drill below.

The decoding strategy that actually works

Do not translate — chunk. For two-digit numbers, catch the units first ("siebenund..."), hold them, then attach the tens: 7… 40 → 47. With practice the chunking becomes automatic and the inversion stops costing you a beat. For hundreds and thousands, listen for the anchors: "hundert" and "tausend" split the word into manageable pieces ("eintausend | neunhundert | fünfundachtzig"). For prices, the word "Euro" is your anchor — everything before it is euros, everything after is cents, and "neunundneunzig" at the end of a price is so common you will learn to expect it. For years, just know the pattern: German always says the full thousands form, so "1985" is one long word and your job is to catch "neunzehnhundert" as a single chunk meaning 19xx.

The famous traps, one by one

Some number pairs deserve special attention because they cause real misunderstandings. sechzehn vs sechzig (16 vs 60): the teen drops the s ("sech-zehn"), the ten keeps it ("sech-zig") — listen for whether the middle is soft "tsayn" or hard "tsig". siebzehn vs siebzig (17 vs 70): same game with sieben losing its "en". In a noisy Bahnhof, "siebzehn" and "siebzig" are genuinely close; the drill forces you to decide fast. dreißig vs vierzig (30 vs 40): easy once you hear it — ß is a long hiss, z is a "ts" snap — but learners who read "30" as "dreizig" in their heads never notice the difference. The inversion family: 21/12 ("einundzwanzig" vs "zwölf"), 31/13, 41/14 — whenever the units digit is 1, 2, or 3, the teen and the twenty-something share sounds; slow playback trains the split. Round hundreds: "einhundert" vs "eintausend" — one syllable ("hundert" vs "tausend") decides whether the bill is a hundred or a thousand, so the drill includes 100 vs 1000-style distractors. When a round tricks you, do not just move on: replay it slowly, say the word form out loud, and notice exactly which phoneme carried the meaning. That single habit — naming the phoneme that fooled you — is what turns a quiz score into real-world hearing.

Frequently asked questions

Why does German say the units before the tens?

It is a very old Germanic pattern (English kept it only in 'thirteen' through 'nineteen'). So 47 is 'siebenundvierzig' — seven-and-forty. Your ear has to hold the units while the tens arrive, which is exactly what this drill trains.

What is the difference between sechzehn and siebzig?

Both drop letters: sechs → sechzehn (16), sieben → siebzig (70). At speed, 16 vs 60 and 17 vs 70 are the most misheard pairs in German — the drill's distractors target exactly these confusions.

How do you say years in German?

In full: 1985 is 'eintausendneunhundertfünfundachtzig', never 'nineteen eighty-five' style pairing. 2000 is 'zweitausend', 2026 'zweitausendsechsundzwanzig'. This drill includes a dedicated year round type.

How are prices spoken in German?

Euros, then 'Euro', then cents: 3,99 € is 'drei Euro neunundneunzig'. Note the comma in writing (3,99 not 3.99) and that round prices still name the cents: 199,00 € is 'einhundertneunundneunzig Euro'.

I keep mixing up 30 and 40. Any trick?

dreißig has the sharp ß (like 'ss'), vierzig has a z. They sound quite different once you listen for it — sss vs ts. Replay those rounds slowly and exaggerate the difference out loud.
