Konjunktiv I: the mood of reported speech

Konjunktiv I (K1) is the mood German uses for indirect speech — reporting what someone else said, claimed, or thought without vouching for it yourself. Der Minister sagte, die Lage sei stabil. The minister may be right or wrong; the K1 form sei signals that these are his words, not the reporter's assertion. This is why K1 dominates news reporting, journalism, academic citation, and formal documentation: it is the grammatical equivalent of quotation marks.

The rule is simple to state: whenever you report speech with a verb like sagen, erklären, behaupten, berichten, meinen, put the reported clause's verb in Konjunktiv I. Sie sagte, sie habe keine Zeit. Er behauptete, er habe nichts gewusst. The dass is optional — Er sagte, dass er krank sei and Er sagte, er sei krank are both correct, and the dass-less version is the more elegant journalistic standard.

How K1 is formed

Take the infinitive stem (infinitive minus -en) and add the K1 endings: -e, -est, -e, -en, -et, -en. For regular verbs this is mechanical: spielen → ich spiele, du spielest, er spiele, wir spielen, ihr spielet, sie spielen. Note the problem immediately: most of these forms are identical to the indicative (ich spiele = ich spiele). German has a built-in escape hatch for exactly this case, covered below.

The three verbs where K1 really matters have distinctive forms worth memorising cold:

The ambiguity rule: when K1 is replaced by K2

Here is the elegant part of the system. K1 exists to mark reported speech, so when a K1 form is identical to the indicative, it fails at its job — the listener cannot hear the reporting. In that case German substitutes Konjunktiv II. The classic case: Sie sagten, sie spielten Tennis. K1 would be sie spielen — indistinguishable from indicative — so K2 spielten steps in. Similarly wir haben (K1) looks like indicative, so Sie sagten, sie hätten keine Zeit uses K2 hätten.

Memorise the fallback chain: K1 → if ambiguous, K2 → if K2 is also ambiguous (or archaic), würde + infinitive. Er sagte, er würde kommen. This chain is the single most tested K1 concept in C1 exams: given an ambiguous form, students must reach for the next link.

K1 in the perfect and future

Compound tenses put the auxiliary in K1 and keep the participle or infinitive unchanged. Perfect: Sie sagte, sie habe das Buch gelesen. Er sagte, er sei noch nie in Berlin gewesen. (Note: sein-verbs take sei, haben-verbs take habe — the auxiliary follows the same haben/sein rule as the indicative perfect.) Future: Er sagte, er werde morgen kommen. Passive: Der Sprecher sagte, das Problem werde gelöst.

Indirect commands and wishes

K1 also handles indirect imperatives and formal wishes: Er sagte, ich solle vorsichtig sein. (He told me to be careful — note the modal sollen in K1.) Der Arzt sagte, der Patient solle sich schonen. Formal wishes use the famous fossilised forms: Es lebe der König! Gott sei Dank. Man nehme … (the recipe formula). These are K1's oldest surviving uses.

K1 vs. indicative in real news German

Read any Tagesschau transcript and watch the mood do its work: Der Kanzler betonte, Deutschland stehe fest an der Seite seiner Partner. Die Polizei teilte mit, der Täter sei noch flüchtig. The reporter never asserts these claims — K1 keeps the attribution visible in the grammar itself. Compare what happens with the indicative: Der Minister sagte, die Lage ist stabil reads as the reporter endorsing the claim. In academic writing the same discipline applies when citing sources: Müller (2020) argumentiert, dass die Datenlage unzureichend sei.

Common mistakes

  • Using indicative in reported speech: Er sagte, er ist krank — wrong in formal German; K1 sei is required (colloquial speech often drops it, exams never do).
  • Forgetting the ambiguity fallback: Sie sagten, sie spielen Tennis — ambiguous K1; use K2 spielten.
  • Perfect K1 with the wrong auxiliary: Er sagte, er habe gegangen — wrong; gehen takes sein: er sei gegangen.
  • Er sagte, er würde krank sein when K1 sei is available and unambiguous — würde is the last resort, not the first.
  • Mixing up the two subjunctives' jobs: K1 reports speech; K2 handles hypotheticals and wishes (drilled in the next tool).

Practice method

Take any news article and convert three direct quotes into indirect speech: „Die Lage ist stabil", sagte der Minister → Der Minister sagte, die Lage sei stabil. Do ten a day for a week and the sei/habe/werde reflex becomes automatic. Then tackle the ambiguity cases deliberately: for each converted sentence, ask whether the K1 form is distinguishable from the indicative — if not, escalate to K2. That judgement call is what separates B2 users from C1 users of German.

Run the quiz above, then read one Tagesschau article circling every K1 form you find — you will be surprised how many there are.

Frequently asked questions

What is Konjunktiv I used for?

Konjunktiv I marks indirect (reported) speech: Er sagte, er sei krank. It signals that the claim is someone else's words, not the speaker's own assertion. It dominates news reporting, academic citation, and formal documentation.

How do you form Konjunktiv I?

Take the infinitive stem and add -e, -est, -e, -en, -et, -en. The key irregular forms are sein (sei, seiest, sei, seien, seiet, seien), haben (habe, habest, habe …), and werden (werde, werdest, werde …).

What happens when Konjunktiv I looks like the indicative?

Use Konjunktiv II instead — the fallback chain is K1 → K2 → würde + infinitive. Example: Sie sagten, sie spielten Tennis (not sie spielen, which is ambiguous).

Is 'dass' required with Konjunktiv I?

No. Both 'Er sagte, dass er krank sei' and the dass-less 'Er sagte, er sei krank' are correct; the dass-less version is the more elegant journalistic standard.

What is the difference between Konjunktiv I and Konjunktiv II?

K1 reports what was said (Er sagte, er sei krank); K2 handles hypotheticals, wishes, and unreal conditions (Wenn er krank wäre, bliebe er zu Hause). They are different moods with different jobs.
