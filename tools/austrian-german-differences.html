Austrian German: a standard, not a dialect

Here's what most learners get wrong: Austrian German (Österreichisches Deutsch) is not a dialect — it's a national standard variety, codified in the Österreichisches Wörterbuch and used in Austrian schools, media, and law. It differs from Germany's Bundesdeutsch mainly in vocabulary (this tool's 30 words), slightly in grammar and pronunciation, and not at all in mutual intelligibility. Austrians understand Tomate perfectly well — they just say Paradeiser. The differences cluster where culture lives: food, daily life, school. This tool maps the 30 most important variants with notes, and the quiz drills both directions — because you'll need to decode Austrian menus and produce the right word when you live there.

Food: where Austria differs most

Austrian menus are the classic culture shock. Paradeiser (tomatoes), Erdäpfel (potatoes — "earth apples," like French pommes de terre), Schlagobers (whipped cream), Marille (apricot — the Wachau valley's pride), Fisolen (green beans), Karfiol (cauliflower, from Italian), Ribisel (redcurrants), Topfen (quark — Topfenstrudel!), Germ (yeast — Germknödel), Staubzucker (powdered sugar, on every Kaiserschmarrn), Powidl (plum jam), Weichsel (sour cherry). Learn these twelve before any trip to Vienna — ordering Aprikosen gets you understood, but ordering Marillen gets you smiled at. Food vocabulary is also the safest Austrian to use: nobody minds a German saying Erdäpfel.

Everyday traps: Sessel, Kasten, Jänner

Daily life brings the false friends. Sessel is the famous trap: in Germany it means armchair; in Austria it's an ordinary chair (Stuhl). Kasten in Austria covers cupboards and wardrobes that Germany splits into Schrank. Jänner replaces Januar entirely — writing Januar on an Austrian form marks you as German instantly. Heuer means "this year" (Heuer fahren wir nach Italien). Pickerl is the inspection sticker on your car (and any sticker). Stiege for stairs, Mistkübel for bin, Nachtkasten for bedside table, Rauchfang for chimney, Trafik for the licensed tobacconist-kiosk. None of these is slang — they're the standard words 9 million people use daily.

Viennese slang: Oida and leiwand

Vienna adds its own slang layer on top of Austrian standard. Oida! (lit. "old one") is the all-purpose address — Oida, schau! — friendly or annoyed depending on tone, like English "dude." Leiwand means great/cool (Des is leiwand!) and has gone mainstream with young Austrians. Hetz is fun (a Hetz haben) — but don't confuse it with German Hetze (agitation/hate campaign), a nasty false friend. Gspusi is a fling, zach means gruelling (Die Prüfung war zach). Viennese slang is for understanding first — deploy Oida only once Viennese friends use it with you, or you'll sound like a tourist performing.

Grammar and spelling: the quiet differences

Beyond vocabulary, Austrian German has its own habits. Perfect tense dominates where Germany might use Präteritum in writing. The "am" + nominalised infinitive progressive (Ich bin am Arbeiten) is standard in Austria and colloquial in Germany. Diminutive -erl (Zuckerl, Pickerl) is standard, not folksy. Spelling: ß is used as in Germany (unlike Switzerland, which dropped it). Address: Austrians keep Sie slightly longer than northern Germans before offering du. None of this blocks communication — but recognising it stops the "that's wrong" reflex that marks the ugly linguistic tourist.

Austrian German in the clinic

For medical German, Austria has its own vocabulary that the Fachsprachenprüfung-adjacent learner must know. Die Rettung is the ambulance service (Germany: Rettungswagen/Rettungsdienst) — die Rettung rufen means call an ambulance. Krankenkassa (not -kasse) is the health insurer. Turnusarzt/Turnusärztin is the junior doctor in rotation (Germany: Assistenzarzt), Primar the department head (Germany: Chefarzt). The Nachtdienst room is the Bereitschaftszimmer; on-call duty is Bereitschaft (tool 48's word in action). Prescriptions: Rezept is the same, but dosage instructions use Austrian phrasing. Elderly Austrian patients speak dialect (tool 53) and expect Austrian standard from you — a German doctor saying Quark to a patient on a Topfen-based diet plan creates confusion, not clarity. If Austria is your target, learn the clinical Austrian alongside the clinical German.

How Austrians hear Germany-German

The relationship is asymmetric: Austrians understand Germany-German effortlessly (German media dominates), while Germans often stare blankly at Erdäpfel. This gives learners in Austria a free pass — speak Bundesdeutsch and you'll be understood everywhere. But it also creates a subtle social dynamic: Austrians notice when you only know the German words, and using the Austrian variant signals respect. You don't need to convert fully — sprinkling in Grüß Gott, Paradeiser, Jänner, and Matura where they fit does the job. Think of it as linguistic good manners: the effort matters more than perfection, and Austrians are famously appreciative when Germans (or learners) bother.

Drill routine

Week one: learn the 12 food words cold — they're the highest-frequency Austrian you'll meet, on every menu. Week two: the everyday traps (Sessel! Jänner! heuer!) and the school words (Matura). Run the 15-round quiz to 12+/15 twice, both directions — decoding Austrian (Paradeiser → ?) matters more than producing it. Then go live: read an Austrian news site (derstandard.at) or a Viennese café menu online and count the Austrian words you now recognise. When Erdäpfel stops looking foreign, Austria has opened up — and with it, 9 million speakers' worth of German you were missing.

Frequently asked questions

Is Austrian German a dialect?

No — it's a national standard variety with its own dictionary (Österreichisches Wörterbuch), used in Austrian schools, media, and law. It differs from Germany's Bundesdeutsch mainly in vocabulary.

What do Austrians call potatoes and tomatoes?

Erdäpfel (potatoes, lit. 'earth apples') and Paradeiser (tomatoes, from 'Paradise apple'). Both are standard Austrian, used on menus nationwide.

What is the 'Sessel' trap?

In Germany, Sessel means armchair; in Austria, Sessel is an ordinary chair (Stuhl). Sitting down on the wrong assumption is a classic German-in-Austria moment.

What does 'Oida' mean in Vienna?

Literally 'old one' — the all-purpose Viennese address, like 'dude' or 'mate.' Friendly or annoyed depending on tone. Use only once Viennese friends use it with you.

What is 'Matura'?

The Austrian school-leaving exam — the equivalent of Germany's Abitur. 'Die Matura bestehen' = to pass the Matura.

Do Austrians understand Germany-German words like 'Tomate'?

Perfectly — the differences are one-directional in practice. Austrians grow up hearing both; Germans in Germany often don't know the Austrian words. That's why decoding (AT → DE) matters most.
