    The complete guide to German plurals

    English plurals are easy: add -s, with a handful of exceptions like children and feet. German works the other way round — the exceptions are the system. A German noun can form its plural in five different ways, and on top of that the stem vowel may change to an umlaut (a → ä, o → ö, u → ü). There is no single rule that predicts which pattern a noun follows, which is why every textbook advises the same thing: learn each noun together with its article and its plural. Memorise der Tisch, die Tische as one unit, not Tisch alone.

    That said, the patterns are not random chaos. Each one has tendencies — typical genders, typical endings — and knowing them turns pure memorisation into educated guessing. Here are the five patterns, each with the examples used in the quiz above.

    Pattern 1: add -e (der Tisch → die Tische)

    This is the most common plural for masculine nouns and for many short neuter nouns. Simply add -e to the singular:

    SingularPluralNotes
    der Tischdie Tischeclassic masculine -e plural
    der Tagdie Tageno umlaut
    der Hunddie Hundeno umlaut
    der Briefdie Briefeno umlaut
    das Jahrdie Jahreneuter nouns often take -e
    das Brotdie Broteneuter -e plural
    das Heftdie Hefteneuter -e plural

    A large subgroup adds -e plus an umlaut. This is one of the trickiest combinations for learners because you must change two things at once:

    SingularPluralNotes

    Pattern 2: add -er with umlaut (das Kind → die Kinder)

    Plurals in -er almost always take an umlaut as well. This pattern belongs mostly to short neuter nouns, with a few masculine ones and exactly one common feminine noun:

    SingularPluralNotes
    das Kinddie Kinderthe textbook example
    das Buchdie Bücheru → ü
    das Hausdie Häuserau → äu
    das Glasdie Gläsera → ä
    das Bilddie Bilderno umlaut possible (i)
    das Eidie Eierno umlaut possible
    das Dorfdie Dörfero → ö
    das Landdie Ländera → ä
    das Wortdie Wörtero → ö
    der Manndie Männermasculine exception
    der Walddie Wäldermasculine, a → ä

    Notice that das Bild → die Bilder and das Ei → die Eier take -er without an umlaut — simply because the vowels i and e cannot take one. The umlaut rule is: only a, o, u and au can change.

    Pattern 3: add -(e)n (die Frau → die Frauen)

    This is the standard plural for feminine nouns — the great majority of them. Nouns already ending in -e just add -n; others add -en:

    SingularPluralNotes
    die Fraudie Frauenadd -en
    die Blumedie Blumenends in -e, add -n
    die Lampedie Lampenends in -e, add -n
    die Tassedie Tassenends in -e, add -n
    die Straßedie Straßenadd -n
    die Uhrdie Uhrenadd -en
    die Schwesterdie Schwesternadd -n

    The same ending appears with weak masculine nouns (a special group that takes -n/-en in every case except the nominative singular): der Student → die Studenten, der Herr → die Herren, der Junge → die Jungen, der Name → die Namen, der Kollege → die Kollegen. A few neuter nouns follow too: das Auge → die Augen, das Bett → die Betten, das Hemd → die Hemden, das Ende → die Enden. Feminine -(e)n plurals never take an umlaut — die Mütter belongs to the next pattern, not this one.

    Pattern 4: add -s (das Auto → die Autos)

    The -s plural will feel familiar to English speakers. In German it is reserved mainly for loanwords and words ending in a vowel:

    SingularPluralNotes

    das Handydie Handysloanword (“mobile phone”)
    das Hoteldie Hotelsloanword
    das Cafédie Cafésloanword, keeps the accent
    das Sofadie Sofasloanword

    der Parkdie Parksloanword
    der Chefdie Chefsloanword
    der Clubdie Clubsloanword

    This is the only pattern where English intuition helps — but beware false friends like das Handy, which means “mobile phone”, not something convenient.

    Pattern 5: no change (das Fenster → die Fenster)

    Masculine and neuter nouns ending in -er, -en or -el usually keep the identical form. Only the article changes from der/das to die:

    SingularPluralNotes
    der Lehrerdie Lehrerends in -er
    der Fahrerdie Fahrerends in -er
    der Schülerdie Schülerends in -er
    der Computerdie Computerends in -er
    der Kofferdie Kofferends in -er
    das Fensterdie Fensterends in -er
    das Zimmerdie Zimmerends in -er

    SingularPluralNotes

    die Mutterdie Mütterumlaut only
    der Bruderdie Brüderumlaut only
    der Apfeldie Äpfelumlaut only
    der Gartendie Gärtenumlaut only
    der Manteldie Mäntelumlaut only

    The umlaut rules, in one place

    Only four sounds can take an umlaut: a → ä (der Gast → die Gäste), o → ö (der Sohn → die Söhne), u → ü (das Buch → die Bücher), and au → äu (das Haus → die Häuser). The vowels e and i never change, which is why das Bett → die Betten and das Bild → die Bilder have no umlaut. Whether a particular noun takes an umlaut cannot be derived from any rule — der Hund becomes die Hunde but der Mund becomes die Münder. You simply have to know it, which is exactly what the quiz trains.

    Which pattern goes with which noun? Tendencies, not laws

    Gender is your best clue. Feminine nouns take -(e)n in the overwhelming majority of cases (die Frau → die Frauen, die Blume → die Blumen). Masculine nouns most often take -e, with or without umlaut (der Tisch → die Tische, der Stuhl → die Stühle). Neuter nouns split between -er (das Kind → die Kinder) and -e (das Jahr → die Jahre). Nouns ending in -er, -en, -el usually do not change (der Lehrer → die Lehrer), and recent loanwords take -s (das Auto → die Autos).

    Every tendency has exceptions, and the exceptions are frequent enough to matter: die Mutter → die Mütter breaks the feminine -(e)n rule, das Bett → die Betten breaks the neuter -er/-e expectation, and der Mann → die Männer is a masculine noun with an -er plural. Treat the tendencies as a first guess and memorisation as the real answer.

    Common exceptions and traps

    A few nouns have two plurals with different meanings. The most famous is das Wort: die Wörter means separate, individual words (as in a vocabulary list), while die Worte means connected words, a speech or saying. Similarly, die Bank becomes die Banken for financial institutions but die Bänke for benches to sit on; der Strauß becomes die Sträuße for bouquets but die Strauße for ostriches; and der Band becomes die Bände for volumes of a book series but die Bands for music groups. These double plurals are a favourite exam trap.

    Watch out for nouns that look alike but pluralise differently: der Leiter (the leader) → die Leiter (no change), but die Leiter (the ladder) → die Leitern. And remember that the plural article is always die — no exceptions: der Tisch → die Tische, das Kind → die Kinder, die Frau → die Frauen.

    Tips for mastering German plurals

    • Learn the triple: never learn Tisch alone — learn der Tisch, die Tische. Article plus plural, every time, from day one.
    • Say umlauts out loud: Äpfel and Apfel sound different; pronouncing the plural correctly cements the spelling.
    • Use the gender tendencies above as a first guess when you meet a new noun, then verify in a dictionary.
    • Drill in both directions: this quiz asks singular → plural; also practise plural → singular so you recognise forms like die Häuser in reading.
    • Revisit your misses: the quiz lists every word you got wrong at the end — write those triples on flashcards and re-test yourself the next day.

    Frequently asked questions

    How many plural patterns does German have?

    Five main endings — -e, -er, -(e)n, -s, and no change — plus umlaut changes that combine with several of them. The quiz covers all of them across 115 nouns.

    Do all German plurals add an umlaut?

    No. The -er pattern almost always includes one (das Haus → die Häuser), the -e pattern sometimes does (der Stuhl → die Stühle, but der Tisch → die Tische), and -(e)n plurals never do (die Frau → die Frauen).

    What is the plural of “der Tisch” and “das Kind”?

    Der Tisch → die Tische and das Kind → die Kinder. These two are the classic examples teachers use to show that German plurals must be memorised, not derived.

    Why do some German nouns not change in the plural?

    Masculine and neuter nouns ending in -er, -en or -el typically keep the same form: der Lehrer → die Lehrer, das Fenster → die Fenster. Only the article changes to die.

    Is the plural article always “die”?

    Yes — der, die and das all become die in the plural, with no exceptions.

    How can I remember which pattern a noun takes?

    Learn each noun as a triple (article + singular + plural), use gender tendencies as a first guess, and drill regularly — the 20-round quiz on this page samples all patterns so every session trains the full system.
