The golden rule: day first, dots between

German dates run day → month → year, always: 07.10.2026 is 7 October 2026. The dots are not decoration — they separate the fields, and the dot after the day number carries grammatical meaning (see below). English speakers whose home format is MM/DD/YYYY must rebuild the habit from zero, because 10.07.2026 reads as 10 July in Germany, not 7 October. The numeric form uses leading zeros (07.10.2026, never 7.10.2026 in formal writing), which also helps you: two digits, dot, two digits, dot, four digits is instantly recognisable as German even before you read the values. The ordinal dot: 7. means "the seventh"

In German, a dot after a number makes it ordinal — der 7. is "the 7th" (der siebte). This is why every German date carries a dot after the day: 7. Oktober is short for der siebte Oktober. Dropping the dot ("7 Oktober") is one of the most visible learner mistakes; it reads as broken German the way "October 7" without "th" reads as broken English. The rule extends everywhere: floor numbers (im 3. Stock), centuries (im 21. Jahrhundert), rankings, and kings (Heinrich VIII. takes a dot too). Internalise it once — number + dot = ordinal — and a whole family of German conventions clicks into place.

Written dates: no leading zero, capital month

In running text the day drops its leading zero: 7. Oktober 2026, not "07. Oktober 2026". Months are nouns and always capitalised — März, Juni, Oktober — which trips English speakers only for the spelling (März with umlaut, Juni/Juli not "June/July"). Memorise the twelve month names as a chant; they're needed constantly for appointments, forms, and small talk about birthdays. Note the pronunciation traps: Februar keeps its first r, and November/Dezember stress the second syllable. The converter's "written" row shows the exact form — compare it against the numeric row until the two feel like the same date in different clothes.

Am, im, or nothing: the preposition system

German assigns every time unit its preposition, and dates are the clearest case. Specific days and full dates take am (an + dem): am 7. Oktober, am Montag, am Wochenende. Months, seasons, and years take im (in + dem): im Oktober, im Herbst, im Jahr 2026. Bare years take nothing: 2026 war ein gutes Jahr. The classic error is carrying English "in" everywhere — "in 7. Oktober" is wrong, and "on Oktober" thinking produces "am Oktober", equally wrong. The trio is small and closed: learn which unit takes which, and the quiz items on this page will feel trivial. Medical German leans on this heavily — am Aufnahmetag, im Verlauf — so it's Fachsprachenprüfung-relevant too.

Letter dates: place, then "den"

Formal letters and emails open with place + date: Aarhus, den 7. Oktober 2026. The den is accusative — shorthand for "(geschrieben) den 7. Oktober". Two traps: using am here (wrong — letters never take am) and writing the American order. In emails the place is often dropped but den stays: den 7. Oktober 2026. This convention appears in the Bewerbung (job application) tool later in this series — a wrong date line on a German CV cover letter is noticed immediately, so get it right here.

Weekdays: Monday first, and the -s adverb

German calendars start the week on Monday (Montag), and Germans say am Montag for a specific Monday. For repetition, add -s: montags = "on Mondays", always lowercase. Weekend vocabulary matters socially: am Wochenende (on the weekend), unter der Woche (during the week). When weekday and date combine — am Mittwoch, dem 7. Oktober — the date after a weekday takes dative dem, not the letter-style den. That's a fine point the quiz tests; in speech, mixing them up is barely noticed, but in writing it's a clear marker of command.

Spoken dates and the year problem

Spoken out, 7. Oktober 2026 becomes der siebte Oktober zweitausendsechsundzwanzig. Years are the hard part: unlike English "twenty twenty-six", German says the full number — zweitausendsechsundzwanzig. (The numbers-to-words tool in this series drills exactly this.) Before 2000 it was neunzehnhundertsechsundachtzig for 1986 — same logic, "nineteen hundred eighty-six" compressed. Decades work like English: in den Neunzigern (in the nineties), capitalised as a noun. Practise saying your birth year and today's date aloud daily; it's the fastest route to automaticity.

ISO format and digital contexts

You'll meet 2026-10-07 in databases, file names, and some official online forms — the ISO 8601 format, year first with hyphens. It's unambiguous and universally understood in Germany, but it's a technical format, not everyday style: don't write it in a letter or say it aloud. Related digital habit: German software and forms sometimes show TT.MM.JJJJ as a placeholder (Tag.Monat.Jahr) — now you know exactly what each letter stands for. And when a German form asks for Geburtsdatum, it expects TT.MM.JJJJ: day first, dots, four-digit year.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the German date 07.10.2026 and not 10.07.2026?

German order is always day.month.year, so 07.10.2026 is 7 October. The American month-first order does not exist in Germany — 10.07.2026 reads as 10 July.

What does the dot after the day number mean?

It marks an ordinal: 7. = der siebte (the seventh). Every German date needs it — '7 Oktober' without the dot is a visible learner error.

Is it 'am 7. Oktober' or 'im 7. Oktober'?

Am 7. Oktober. Specific days and full dates take 'am'; 'im' is only for months, seasons and years (im Oktober, im Herbst, im Jahr 2026).

How do you write the date on a German letter?

Place + 'den' + date: 'Aarhus, den 7. Oktober 2026'. Never use 'am' in a letter heading — 'den' is the accusative form.

Do Germans write 'October 7, 2026' ever?

No. The written form is '7. Oktober 2026' — no leading zero on the day, capitalised month, ordinal dot. Month names are German: Oktober, März, Juni.
