Stress is the skeleton of German rhythm

German is a stress-timed language: stressed syllables land at roughly even intervals and unstressed syllables get squeezed between them, which is what gives German its characteristic driving rhythm. Put the stress on the wrong syllable and the effect is dramatic — verSTEHen with the stress on ver does not sound like a charming accent, it sounds like a different word that the listener has to decode. English speakers have an advantage here: English is stress-timed too, so the rhythm feels natural. The challenge is purely where the stress falls, and in German that is governed by a small set of rules that cover the overwhelming majority of words. This tool teaches the four rules, gives you a predictor that applies them to any word, and drills 43 words until the patterns are automatic.

Rule 1: native words stress the first syllable

The default is beautifully simple: in words of Germanic origin, the stress falls on the first syllable. WAS-ser, AR-beit, VA-ter, MUT-ter, FAH-ren, LE-sen, AP-fel, FENS-ter, GAR-ten — the pattern never varies. This covers most everyday vocabulary, and it is why German learners should always assume first-syllable stress until a rule says otherwise. The trap is applying it blindly: the moment a word carries one of the prefixes in Rule 2 or one of the foreign endings in Rule 4, the default no longer applies. Try the predictor above on Wasser and verstehen back to back and watch the reasoning change.

Rule 2: be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, ent-, emp- are never stressed

Seven prefixes are always unstressed: be-, ge-, er-, ver-, zer-, ent-, emp-. The stress skips them and lands on the root: be-KOM-men, ver-STEH-en, ent-SCHEI-den, er-ZÄHL-en, ge-WINN-en, emp-FEHL-en, zer-BRECH-en. This single rule fixes the stress of thousands of verbs and nouns, and it is the one English speakers violate most, because English has no equivalent habit — the instinct is to stress the first syllable of verstehen and produce VER-steh-en, which no German would recognise without effort. Note the edge case the predictor handles: in two-syllable words like GEH-en or BE-ten, the ge-/be- is part of the root, not a prefix, so first-syllable stress applies — while ver-BOT, be-SUCH, ge-RÄT keep the prefix unstressed. When in doubt, the dictionary marks stress; the predictor covers the common cases.

Rule 3: separable prefixes ARE stressed

The mirror image of Rule 2: separable prefixes carry the stress. AN-kom-men, AUF-steh-en, MIT-bring-en, VOR-le-sen, ZU-ma-chen, AB-fah-ren, EIN-la-den, NACH-den-ken, FERN-seh-en — the prefix is stressed because it carries the distinctive meaning of the verb. This is the rule that makes stress genuinely meaningful in German, because some verbs exist in both forms with different meanings: UM-fahren (to run something over — separable, stress on um) versus um-ARM-en (to embrace — inseparable, stress on the root). The most famous pair is ÜBER-set-zen (to ferry across) versus über-SETZ-en (to translate). Stress is not decoration here — it is grammar you can hear.

Rule 4: foreign words stress the foreign part

Words borrowed from French, Latin and Greek keep their foreign stress, which usually falls on or near the ending. The patterns are completely regular once you know the endings: -tion/-sion (in-for-ma-TI-on, dis-kus-si-ON), -tät (u-ni-ver-si-TÄT, qua-li-TÄT), -ik (mu-SIK, Lo-GIK), -ieren verbs (stu-DIE-ren, te-le-fo-NIE-ren, mar-SCHIE-ren — always the -ie-), -ie with a long i: (bi-o-lo-GIE, Me-lo-DIE), -ist, -eur, -är, -age (Jour-na-LIST, Fri-SEUR, Se-kre-TÄR, Ga-RA-ge). English speakers often drag these toward English stress patterns — IN-for-ma-tion on the English model — but German keeps the French stress on the ending. One useful contrast: the verb stu-DIE-ren stresses -ie-, while the noun STU-di-um is fully nativised with first-syllable stress.

What the predictor cannot do

Honesty about limits is part of learning. The predictor applies the four rules mechanically, and three cases defeat it. First, ambiguous prefixes: um- and durch- can be separable or inseparable (UM-fahren vs um-ARM-en), and only meaning — or a dictionary — decides. Second, compounds: Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaftskapitän stresses the first element (DO-nau-…), but each element follows its own rules internally, which the simple engine does not model. Third, family words like Fa-MI-lie: the -ilie ending has its own stress that the general -ie rule would miss (the predictor special-cases it, but there are cousins). Use the predictor as a first guess and the word bank as ground truth — and when a word matters, check the dictionary's stress mark.

Mistakes to unlearn

Four habits cover nearly every error. English stress on foreign words (IN-for-ma-tion) — fix by learning the ending patterns in Rule 4. First-syllable stress on prefixed verbs (VER-steh-en, BE-komm-en) — fix with Rule 2; say the prefix quietly and punch the root. Unstressed separable prefixes (an-KOM-men) — fix with Rule 3; the prefix carries the meaning, so it carries the stress. Flat, even stress — some learners, afraid of choosing wrong, stress every syllable equally; German needs the contrast between the stressed syllable and the squashed unstressed ones, so commit to the stress even when guessing. Record yourself reading Der Lehrer versteht die Information and check each word against the bank: LEH-rer, ver-STEHT, in-for-ma-TI-on.

A five-minute daily routine

One minute: clap the stress of ten bank words — one loud clap on the stressed syllable, silent taps on the rest. Physicalising the rhythm locks it in faster than thinking about it. Two minutes: take six words from the bank, two from each rule group, predict the stress with the rules before tapping the audio, and correct yourself aloud. Two minutes: the quiz — listen, then tap the stressed syllable without looking at the spelling. After a week, add the minimal-pair sentences (Er fährt das Kind um vs Er umarmt das Kind) and feel how stress alone carries the meaning. Within two to three weeks the four rules become reflex, and your German gains the rhythm that makes listeners relax — the surest sign of comprehensible pronunciation.

Regional notes

Word stress is one of the most uniform features of German — the four rules hold across Germany, Austria and Switzerland with barely any variation. You will occasionally hear Austrian speakers stress French loans slightly differently (Ka-FEE for Kaffee is standard everywhere, but some Austrian -ik words vary), and Swiss German keeps the same stress patterns with its own vowel colours. For learners this is good news: unlike the r or the ch sounds, stress has no regional minefield. Learn the standard patterns and they work everywhere German is spoken.

Frequently asked questions

Is German word stress always on the first syllable?

For native Germanic words, yes — Wasser, Arbeit, fahren all stress the first syllable, and that covers most everyday vocabulary. But unstressed prefixes (be-, ver-, ent-), separable prefixes (an-, auf-, mit-) and foreign endings (-tion, -ieren, -tät) all override the default, which is why a predictor beats a single rule.

How can I tell whether a prefix is separable?

Three tests. The meaning test: separable prefixes add a concrete directional meaning (an-kommen = come + toward). The sentence test: in a main clause the prefix goes to the end — Er kommt morgen an. And the dictionary test: separable verbs are marked 'trennbar'. When in doubt, assume common prefixes like an-, auf-, aus-, ein-, mit-, vor-, zu- are separable.

Why do “umfahren” and “umarmen” stress different syllables?

Because um- can be separable or inseparable, and the stress tells you which. UM-fahren (separable, stress on um) means to run something over; um-ARM-en (inseparable, stress on the root) means to embrace. The famous pair is ÜBER-set-zen (ferry across) versus über-SETZ-en (translate) — stress alone changes the meaning.

Will people still understand me if my stress is wrong?

Usually, but at a cost. Wrong stress forces the listener to decode the word consciously instead of recognising it instantly, which is tiring over a whole conversation — and in professional settings like the clinic it reads as unprepared. The good news: four rules cover the vast majority of words, so the fix is cheap.

Why is it “stuDIEren” but “STUdium”?

Because they followed different paths into German. Studieren kept its French verb ending -ieren, which always stresses the -ie-. Studium was fully nativised as a Latin neuter noun centuries ago and took normal first-syllable stress. The pair is a perfect demo of Rule 4 versus Rule 1 — same root, different history, different stress.
