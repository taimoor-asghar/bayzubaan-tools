The checklist

Tick every statement that is honestly true for you — not what you could do with a dictionary and an hour, but what you can do now, unaided. 25 statements, A1 → C1. Your estimated writing level and gap analysis appear at the end.

Why can-do checklists beat vague levels

Ask a learner "is your writing B1?" and you'll get a shrug. Ask "can you write a formal email to your landlord, unaided?" and you'll get the truth. The CEFR was designed around can-do statements for exactly this reason: levels are abstractions, but I can write a for-and-against text is a verifiable fact. This self-assessment turns the 25 writing can-dos from A1 to C1 into a checklist, a level estimate, and — most usefully — a gap profile. The profile matters more than the headline: two learners can both be "B1" while one can't write a formal email and the other can't narrate the past. Same label, completely different homework.

How the scoring works (and why it's strict)

A level counts as solid only when you tick at least 4 of its 5 statements. Your estimated level is the highest level that is solid and where every lower level has at least 3 of 5 ticked — the contiguity rule. Why so strict? Because writing is cumulative: you cannot write a B2 essay if your A2 sentence-connecting is shaky, and the checklist catches exactly that. If you tick 5/5 at B1 but only 2/5 at A2, the tool caps you below — not to punish you, but because the A2 gap (probably basic connectors or past narration) will sabotage everything above it. Fix the foundation first; the higher skills come faster on solid ground. The one non-negotiable rule: tick only what you can do unaided, right now. "I could do it with a dictionary and an hour" doesn't count — exams and real life don't offer either.

Reading your gap profile

After scoring, you get five bars — one per level — coloured solid green (4–5/5), amber developing (3/5), or red gap (0–2/5). Read them like a doctor reads blood work. Red at A1/A2 with green above: rare, but it means you've been writing above your foundations — probably memorised essay phrases without the grammar underneath. Go back and fill the red; it's quick and it unlocks everything. Green up to B1, red at B2: the classic plateau. Your B2 to-do list is the five B2 statements — treat them as a syllabus, not a wish list. Amber everywhere: you're a generalist; pick the lowest amber level and turn it green first. All green to C1: stop assessing and start producing — long-form writing with feedback is the only remaining lever.

The five writing skills that actually separate the levels

1. Sentence connection (A2). The jump from isolated sentences to und/aber/denn/weil/dass text. If your writing is a list of short sentences, this is the fix — one week of connector drills transforms readability. 2. Paragraph logic (B1). One idea per paragraph, reasons attached to opinions. Most B1 exam failures are paragraph failures, not grammar failures. 3. Connector range (B2). deshalb is B1; hingegen, zudem, insofern, dennoch is B2. Examiners literally scan for range — the linking-words trainer exists for this. 4. Register control (B2). Knowing when du becomes Sie, when contractions die, when the passive appears. Formal writing has rules; learn them as rules. 5. Syntactic density (C1). Nominalisations (die Durchführung instead of durchführen), extended attributes (die in den letzten Jahren stark gestiegene Nachfrage). This is what makes C1 text feel C1 — and it's trainable by rewriting your own B2 sentences denser.

A monthly writing routine that works

1. Diagnose (10 min): retake this checklist; note which bar moved. 2. Target (5 min): pick the single lowest unticked statement at your frontier level — e.g. "I can summarise a text in my own words." 3. Produce (30 min, weekly): write 150–250 words targeting exactly that skill. Summarising? Summarise a Tagesschau article. Formal email? Write the complaint letter you'd actually send. 4. Compare (15 min): run your text through the reading-difficulty analyser — is its band rising toward your target? 5. Correct (ongoing): keep an error log. Not "grammar mistakes" vaguely, but the three specific errors you actually make (articles? verb position? adjective endings?) — then drill those tools. Learners who write 200 words a week with this loop outpace learners who "study writing" for hours without producing a sentence.

Writing and the other skills

Writing is the slowest skill to build and the most honest mirror of your grammar: you can't fake verb position on paper the way you can mumble past it in speech. That's why writing practice upgrades everything else — the grammar you can produce correctly in writing becomes available in speech within weeks. Pair this assessment with the overall level estimator: if your writing lags your reading by more than a band, you're under-producing (write more); if it leads, you're probably over-ticking (be stricter). And when a level's five statements are all genuinely ticked, prove it: take that level's placement test. The checklist says what you can do; the placement test verifies it under pressure.

Common self-assessment traps

The dictionary trap: "I can write a formal email" — with DeepL open. Doesn't count. The one-time trap: you did it once in a course, six months ago. Skills decay; tick only current ability. The recognition trap: you recognise Konjunktiv I in a text, so you tick "I can use it." Recognition is reading; the statement says writing. The modesty trap (rarer): you can do it but won't tick it because it "wasn't perfect." The standard is communicative success, not perfection — a B1 email with two article errors still counts as written. When in doubt, test yourself: set a timer, write the thing, then judge. Ten minutes of honest testing beats ten minutes of wondering.

Frequently asked questions

How honest should I be on the can-do checklist?

Brutally. Tick only what you can do unaided, right now — no dictionary, no translator, no hour of preparation. Inflated ticks just hide the gaps this tool is designed to find. It's for you, not an examiner.

Why did I get capped below my best level?

The contiguity rule: a level only counts if all lower levels have at least 3/5 ticked. Writing is cumulative — shaky A2 connectors will sabotage B2 essays. Fill the lower gap first; it's usually quick and unlocks everything above.

My writing level is lower than my placement test level. Is that normal?

Yes — writing usually lags reading by about half a band because production is harder than recognition. A gap bigger than one full band means you're under-producing: write 200 words a week targeting your frontier statements.

How do I turn an unticked statement into a ticked one?

Treat each statement as a syllabus item: produce real text targeting exactly that skill (e.g. summarise an article for the B2 summarising statement), then verify — run it through the reading-difficulty analyser and check the band.

How often should I retake the assessment?

Monthly. Watch the five level bars, not just the headline — a bar moving from red to amber is real progress even when the headline level hasn't changed yet.

Can this replace a formal writing exam?

No — it's a diagnostic, not a certificate. Use it to find gaps and track progress, then prove each level with that level's placement test and, when it counts, a real exam (Goethe, telc, ÖSD).
