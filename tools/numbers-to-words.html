The German number system at a glance

German numbers 0–12 are unique words you simply memorise (null, eins, zwei … zwölf), 13–19 mostly attach -zehn to the unit (dreizehn, vierzehn), and the tens from 20 to 90 have their own forms. From 21 upward, German flips the order English speakers expect: you say the unit first, then the ten, joined by und. So 21 is einundzwanzig — literally "one-and-twenty" — exactly like old-fashioned English "four-and-twenty". Once you accept the reversal, the system is extremely regular.

The spelling traps: sech-, sieb-, dreißig

Three quirks cause most errors. First, 16 is sechzehn and 60 is sechzig — the -s- of sechs disappears (never "sechszehn" or "sechszig"). Second, 17 is siebzehn and 70 is siebzig — the -en of sieben disappears (never "siebenzehn" or "siebenzig"). Third, 30 is dreißig — the only ten written with ß, and with no -z- (never "dreissig" in standard German orthography, never "dreißzig"). The converter above encodes all three rules, and the quiz drills them hard because they are the words that betray learners in real conversations.

Ein vs eins

The word for "one" changes shape by context: eins is the standalone counting form ("eins, zwei, drei …"), while ein is used in compounds — einundzwanzig (21), einhundert (100), eintausend (1,000). And einhunderteins (101) shows both: the hundred takes ein, the final unit takes eins. Before masculine/neuter nouns, ein also carries the article endings you know from adjective declension (einundzwanzig Äpfel, einhundertein Euro — the -n appears before masculine accusative nouns).

Hundreds, thousands, and the missing "und"

A frequent English-speaker error is inserting und where German has none. 101 is einhunderteins — no und between hundred and unit (English "one hundred and one" does not translate). Likewise 250 is zweihundertfünfzig and 1,050 is eintausendfünfzig. The und appears only inside the tens compound: neunundneunzig, fünfundvierzig. Everything else joins directly, producing the famous long words like eintausendneunhundertneunundneunzig — still a single word, still one compound, no spaces, no hyphens.

From a million up: numbers become nouns

At 1,000,000 the system changes: Million, Milliarde (billion), and Billion (trillion) are capitalised nouns, written as separate words and declined — eine Million, zwei Millionen, mit einer Million Euro. This is a genuine false-friend zone: English "billion" (10⁹) is German Milliarde, and German Billion means 10¹². In business and medical contexts, mixing these up is a real error, so learn the scale deliberately.

Numbers in real life: years, prices, phone numbers

Years are read as plain numbers: 2026 is zweitausendsechsundzwanzig (older centuries often split: 1999 can be neunzehnhundertneunundneunzig — "nineteen hundred ninety-nine" style — in dates, though the full form is always correct). Prices: €4,99 is vier Euro neunundneunzig. Phone numbers are usually read digit by digit — and note that many Germans say zwo instead of zwei on the phone for clarity, though zwei is the standard written form. Times pair with the time-telling drill: 14:30 is vierzehn Uhr dreißig in official style, halb drei in everyday speech.

Ordinal numbers and fractions

Cardinal numbers become ordinals (first, second, third) with -te for 1–19 and -ste from 20 up: der erste, der dritte (note the irregular stem), der achte (one t), der zwanzigste. The irregulars to memorise are erste (1st), dritte (3rd), achte (8th) — everything else is mechanical. Ordinals decline like adjectives (am dritten März, zum ersten Mal), which the date drill covers in depth. Fractions: ein Drittel (a third), ein Viertel (a quarter), die Hälfte (half) — but note that "half past two" is halb drei, not "halb nach zwei", a trap the time-telling quiz drills specifically.

Study strategy

Start by typing 0–30 into the converter and reading each word aloud — the teens and tens must be automatic before compounds make sense. Then convert your own meaningful numbers: your birth year, your phone number, prices from a German supermarket flyer. Drill the three spelling traps (sechzehn/sechzig, siebzehn/siebzig, dreißig) with the quiz until you stop hesitating — hesitation on numbers in conversation reads as uncertainty about the language itself. Finally, practise the long compounds out loud: eintausendneunhundertneunundneunzig said fluently is one of the most satisfying moments in German learning, and it trains the compound-swallowing rhythm the whole language runs on.

Frequently asked questions

How do you say 16, 17, 60, 70 in German?

16 is sechzehn and 60 is sechzig — the -s- of sechs drops. 17 is siebzehn and 70 is siebzig — the -en of sieben drops. These are the most misspelled numbers in German; learn them as exceptions.

Why is 21 'einundzwanzig' — why does German reverse the order?

German puts the unit before the ten: einundzwanzig (one-and-twenty). This matches old English ('four-and-twenty blackbirds'). The reversal applies to all compounds 21–99, joined by und with no spaces.

Is 101 'einhunderteins' or 'einhundertundeins'?

Einhunderteins — there is no und between hundred and unit. The und only joins tens compounds (neunundneunzig). Inserting und after hundert or tausend is a classic English-speaker error.

What's the difference between ein and eins?

Eins is the standalone counting word; ein is used in compounds (einundzwanzig, einhundert, eintausend). Note einhunderteins (101) uses both: ein- for the hundred, -eins for the unit.

How do you say 1,000,000 in German?

Eine Million — from a million upward German uses capitalised nouns: eine Million, zwei Millionen, eine Milliarde (10⁹). Warning: English 'billion' is German Milliarde; German Billion means 10¹².
