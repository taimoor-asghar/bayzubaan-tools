Why German strong verbs feel hard — and how to tame them

German has around 170 strong (irregular) verbs, and they include almost every verb you actually use daily: gehen, kommen, sehen, essen, trinken, sprechen, lesen, schreiben, geben, nehmen. You cannot sidestep them. The good news is that strong verbs are not random. They fall into ablaut families — vowel-change patterns shared across dozens of verbs. Learn the family, and you get the whole clan for free.

Every strong verb is summarised by its principal parts: infinitive, 3rd-person singular present, Präteritum, Partizip II. sprechen — spricht — sprach — gesprochen. If you know these four forms, you can build every tense: the present from spricht, the past from sprach, the perfect from gesprochen. Dictionaries print exactly these four for a reason.

The seven ablaut families

The classical grammar divides strong verbs into seven classes by their vowel sequence (infinitive → Präteritum → Partizip II):

  • ei – i – i: bleiben–blieb–geblieben, schreiben–schrieb–geschrieben, leiden–litt–gelitten. The most regular family: once you know one, the pattern is locked.
  • ie – o – o: fliegen–flog–geflogen, schießen–schoss–geschossen, frieren–fror–gefroren. Note the doubled consonant after a short vowel (schoss, not schos).
  • i – a – u: singen–sang–gesungen, trinken–trank–getrunken, finden–fand–gefunden, binden–band–gebunden. Probably the largest family — very productive.
  • e – a – o: sprechen–sprach–gesprochen, nehmen–nahm–genommen, stehlen–stahl–gestohlen, befehlen–befahl–befohlen.
  • e – a – e: lesen–las–gelesen, sehen–sah–gesehen, geschehen–geschah–geschehen. The participle keeps the infinitive vowel — a memory hook.
  • a – u – a: fahren–fuhr–gefahren, tragen–trug–getragen, graben–grub–gegraben, schlagen–schlug–geschlagen.
  • au – ie – au: laufen–lief–gelaufen, saufen–soff–gesoffen. Small but high-frequency.

Inside each family there are spelling quirks — ß alternates with ss (aß/ass, vergaß/vergaß stays ß), short vowels double the following consonant (litt, schoss, soff) — but the vowel skeleton holds.

The 3rd-person singular trap

The second principal part exists because of one specific quirk: verbs whose stem vowel is e or ä change to i or ie in the du/er forms (sprechen→spricht, sehen→sieht, essen→isst, vergessen→vergisst), and verbs with stem a or au often umlaut to ä/äu (fahren→fährt, laufen→läuft, schlafen→schläft, waschen→wäscht, fallen→fällt, fangen→fängt, tragen→trägt). Verbs with other stem vowels do not change (kommen→kommt, trinken→trinkt). This is the form learners most often get wrong in speech, so the quiz above drills it explicitly.

How to memorise them efficiently

Do not learn verbs alphabetically — learn them by family. Take the i–a–u family and drill singen, trinken, finden, binden, springen together; the shared rhythm anchors all of them. Second, always learn the four principal parts as one chant: "sprechen, spricht, sprach, gesprochen" — rhythm is a memory device. Third, use the verbs in the Perfekt in daily narration (ich habe gegessen, ich bin gegangen), because the participle is the form you will say most often.

Watch out for false regularity: bringen–brachte–gebracht and denken–dachte–gedacht look strong (vowel change) but take weak -te endings — they are "mixed" verbs. And a handful of verbs have both forms in different meanings: hängen (intransitive, strong: hing, gehangen) vs. hängen (transitive, weak: hängte, gehängt).

The compound multiplier: one pattern, many verbs

Here is the fact that halves your memorisation load: prefixed compounds inherit the base verb's strong pattern. Learn stehen — stand — gestanden and you automatically know aufstehen (stand auf, aufgestanden), verstehen (verstand, verstanden), bestehen (bestand, bestanden) and widerstehen. Learn schlagen — schlug — geschlagen and you get vorschlagen, zuschlagen, aufschlagen. Learn sehen — sah — gesehen and fernsehen, ansehen, aussehen, zusehen follow for free, with the ge- tucked after the separable prefix (ferngesehen, angesehen). The ~170 strong verbs therefore generate many hundreds of compounds at zero extra cost.

This is also why the separable-verb quiz and this trainer reinforce each other: the prefix tells you about word order, the stem tells you about the vowel. When you meet an unfamiliar compound like unterbrechen, strip the prefix, find brechen — brach — gebrochen, and you have unterbrach and unterbrochen immediately. Intermediate learners who think in compounds like this stop memorising verb-by-verb and start seeing the system.

Reading real German with strong verbs

In written German — newspapers, novels, the Fachsprachenprüfung texts — the Präteritum of strong verbs appears constantly, and it is worth training your eye for it. The bare stem with no ending in the ich/er form is the giveaway: er ging, sie sah, es begann. In a news sentence like "Der Minister trat zurück und sprach von einem Neuanfang", trat and sprach are the only past markers; recognising them as treten and sprechen keeps your reading speed up.

Also watch for verbs that change meaning with the prefix: umfahren (to drive around, weak: umfuhr? no — umfahren with separable prefix um- means "to knock down": er fuhr ihn um). Stress is the audible clue (UMfahren vs. umFAHren), and in writing you must simply know which verb you are looking at. Frequently asked questions

What are the four principal parts of sprechen?

sprechen — spricht — sprach — gesprochen: infinitive, 3rd-person singular present, Präteritum, Partizip II. These four forms generate every tense of the verb.

Why does 'essen' become 'isst' in the third person?

Strong verbs with stem vowel e change it to i or ie in the du and er/sie/es forms: essen→isst, sprechen→spricht, sehen→sieht, vergessen→vergisst. This is the e→i ablaut, one of the oldest patterns in German.

What is the difference between strong, weak and mixed verbs?

Weak verbs form the past with -te and never change the vowel (machen–machte–gemacht). Strong verbs change the vowel and take no -te ending (sprechen–sprach–gesprochen). Mixed verbs do both: vowel change plus -te (bringen–brachte–gebracht, denken–dachte–gedacht).

How many strong verbs are there in German?

About 170 in the modern standard language, but a small core of roughly 40 covers the vast majority of everyday usage. This trainer drills the 20 most frequent.

Is 'aß' or 'ass' correct for the past of essen?

Both spellings represent the same word: aß is the standard spelling after a long vowel; ass is the Swiss spelling (which has no ß) and is accepted as equivalent. This quiz accepts either.
