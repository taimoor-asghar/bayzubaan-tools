One spelling, three sounds

The letters ch in German stand for three completely different sounds, and using the wrong one is one of the most audible learner mistakes: ich said with a hard [k] (“ick”) or Buch said with [ç] (“Büch”) marks you instantly. The three sounds are the ich-Laut [ç] (a breathy palatal hiss, as in ich, mich, nicht), the ach-Laut [x] (a throaty fricative, as in ach, Bach, Buch), and a plain [k] in words of Greek origin (as in Chor, Charakter, Chaos). The good news: which sound appears is almost entirely predictable from a single rule.

The one-letter rule: look left of the ch

Find the ch in the word and look at the letter immediately before it. If that letter is a, o, u or the diphthong au, the sound is the ach-Laut [x]: Bach, noch, Buch, auch, rauchen. In every other position — after e, i, ä, ö, ü, after diphthongs like ei, eu, after a consonant (l, n, r), or at the start of the word — the sound is the ich-Laut [ç]: ich, echt, Milch, durch, Bücher, feucht. Try it on the predictor above: it applies exactly this rule, and it is right for the overwhelming majority of German words.

Shaping the ich-Laut [ç]

The ich-Laut is not the English “sh” — that is the single most common substitution and it sounds wrong to every native ear. Instead, think of the breathy “h” at the start of English “huge” or “human”: the tongue rises close to the hard palate and air hisses through a narrow channel. Now hold that tongue position and voice it into a word: huuuuge → ich. The sound is soft, high, and forward in the mouth. Practice chain: huge – ich – mich – nicht – Milch, feeling the tongue stay in the same forward position for all of them.

Shaping the ach-Laut [x]

The ach-Laut lives further back: the back of the tongue approaches the soft palate (the velum) and air scrapes through. English has no exact equivalent, but three anchors get you there: the ch in Scottish “loch”, the j in Spanish Juan, and the feeling of clearing your throat very gently. Start from a soft throat-clear (“ahem” without the voice), then shape it into ach – Bach – Buch – noch – auch. The classic learner error is overdoing it into a harsh cough; the real [x] is quiet and controlled, more like warm breath on a cold window than a throat infection.

The [k] words: Greek roots

A small, closed set of words pronounces ch as a plain [k]: Chor, Charakter, Chaos, Christ, Chlor, Chrom, Orchester, Architekt. Almost all come from Greek, and almost all start with ch or chr. There is no rule to derive — just learn the list, and note the trap inside Architekt: the first ch is [k] (Greek root) but the second ch follows an i and is therefore [ç] ([aʁçiˈtɛkt]). When in doubt about an unfamiliar ch-word, the one-letter rule is still your best first guess; only memorised Greek words override it.

The -ig ending: north says [ç], south says [k]

Words ending in -ig — König, ruhig, zwanzig, lustig, schwierig — are pronounced with [ç] in standard (northern) German: Köni[ç]. In southern Germany and Austria you will commonly hear [k] (Könik), which is an accepted regional variant, not a mistake. For learners aiming at the standard — and for the Fachsprachenprüfung — the [ç] version is the safe choice. Note the spelling trap: -lich (freundlich) is always [ç] too, following the same “after i” logic.

Bonus rule: chs → [ks]

When ch is followed by s, the combination collapses to [ks]: sechs [zɛks] (six), wachsen [ˈvaksn̩] (to grow), der Wechsel (change). Do not try to pronounce the [x] and the [s] separately — it is simply “ks” as in English “box”. This one rule fixes a whole family of number and verb words at once.

Mistakes to unlearn

Four substitutions cover nearly every learner error. “sh” for [ç] (misht for nicht) — fix with the “huge” anchor. [k] for [ç] (“ick” for ich) — this is actually the Berlin dialect, charming in Berlin, wrong in an exam. [ç] for [x] (“Büch” for Buch) — fix with the throat-clear anchor. English “ch” as in “church” for any German ch — German ch is never [ʧ]. Run the word bank above group by group, exaggerate the tongue positions for a week, and the three sounds separate permanently.

A five-minute daily routine

One minute: alternate ich – ach – ich – ach slowly, feeling the tongue slide forward and back. Two minutes: take five words from the bank, predict the sound with the rule before tapping the audio, and check yourself. Two minutes: the quiz. After a week, add the -ig words and the Greek list. Most learners find the rule clicks within days; the muscle memory for [ç] versus [x] takes two to three weeks of daily short sessions. Test yourself the honest way: record ten words, wait a day, then listen and label each ch without looking.

Frequently asked questions

Is the ich-Laut the same as English “sh”?

No, and this is the most common mistake. English “sh” [ʃ] is made with the tongue tip near the ridge behind the teeth; the ich-Laut [ç] is made with the middle of the tongue near the hard palate, like the breathy “h” in “huge”. They sound clearly different to native ears — “misht” for “nicht” is immediately recognisable as a learner error.

Why do some Germans say “Könik” instead of “Könich”?

That is the southern German and Austrian pronunciation of the -ig ending. The standard (northern) pronunciation taught in textbooks and expected in exams is [ç]: Köni[ç]. Both are correct German; learners should default to the standard form.

How is 'ch' pronounced in Chemie?

With the ich-Laut: [çeˈmiː]. Chemie is not a Greek-ch word despite the spelling — the ch follows the word-initial rule and takes [ç]. The same goes for China ([ˈçiːna] in standard German).

Why does ch sound different after 'a' than after 'i'?

It is coarticulation: the tongue anticipates the neighbouring vowel. After the open back vowel 'a', the tongue is already low and back, so the fricative comes out throaty ([x]); after the close front vowel 'i', the tongue is high and forward, so it comes out palatal ([ç]). The spelling rule simply records what the mouth does naturally.

My ach-Laut sounds like coughing. How do I soften it?

Use less air and less constriction: think of fogging a mirror with warm breath rather than clearing your throat. Start from a whispered “haaa”, slowly raise the back of the tongue until you hear friction, and stop the moment the sound appears. The real [x] in normal speech is quiet — the harsh version only appears when learners push too hard.
