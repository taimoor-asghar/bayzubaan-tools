What A2 means (and what this test checks)

A2 (Elementary) is where German gets serious: you can handle routine matters, describe your background in simple past, and survive most everyday situations. Concretely, A2 adds to A1: Präteritum (narrative past), reflexive verbs, two-way prepositions with case logic, comparatives/superlatives, Konjunktiv II (würde + infinitive, hätte/wäre), relative clauses, genitive basics, adjective endings across declensions, subordinate word order, passive recognition, and infinitives with zu. This test's 25 questions sample all of it — each tagged with its topic, so your result is a diagnosis, not just a score.

A2 to B1: the bridge

B1 is the independence threshold — the level employers and universities ask for. The bridge from A2: full subordinate clause repertoire (obwohl, damit, falls, als ob), Konjunktiv II past (hätte gemacht), passive in all tenses, relative clauses with prepositions (mit dem ich…), n-declension, and vocabulary past 2,000 words. Common A2 traps (read before the test)

A2 has classic traps this test deliberately includes. Wäre vs würde: wenn ich Zeit hätte (not *würde haben) — haben and sein take the old Konjunktiv forms, everything else takes würde. Relative pronouns: der Mann, den ich sah — the pronoun's case comes from its role in the relative clause (ich sah den Mann → accusative den), not from the main clause. Two-way prepositions: position = dative (auf dem Tisch), direction = accusative (auf den Tisch) — say wo? or wohin? before choosing. Separable + zu: anzurufen, not *zu anrufen — the zu slots between prefix and stem. Adjective endings: dative plural is always -n (mit guten Freunden) — the one ending with no exceptions. Knowing these five traps cold is worth 5+ points.

Why A2 is the make-or-break level

More learners quit at A2 than at any other level — and it's not the grammar's fault. A1 is novelty: everything is new, progress is visible daily. A2 is the plateau: the easy wins are gone, the remaining grammar (Konjunktiv II, relative clauses, passive) feels abstract, and real conversations still outrun you. The result is the classic A2 slump: study hours stay flat while motivation drains. This test fights the slump two ways. First, it makes progress visible — a 14→18 jump over a month is undeniable proof the work is working, the kind of evidence motivation runs on. Second, it makes the work specific: "study German" is paralysing, but "fix two-way prepositions this week" is a Tuesday evening. Learners who test monthly quit far less than learners who don't — not because the test teaches, but because it converts vague effort into a scoreboard. If you're in the A2 slump right now, take the test today: whatever the score, you'll leave with a plan instead of a mood.

After the test: your study loop

Test → study the top 2 missed topics for two weeks → retest. Don't study everything: fixing your two weakest topics moves the score fastest. Pair each topic with its tool and drill it daily — fifteen focused minutes beat an hour of unfocused review. When you pass A2 (20+), take the B1 placement test the same week. Levels are meant to be climbed, not camped on. And remember: the score is a snapshot, not a sentence — every retake is a new chance to prove the work.
