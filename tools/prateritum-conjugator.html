Conjugate in the Präteritum

Weak verbs: stem + -te

Regular (weak) verbs form the Präteritum with total predictability: add -te to the stem, then the endings -∅, -st, -∅, -n, -t, -n. Machen gives the textbook table: ich machte, du machtest, er machte, wir machten, ihr machtet, sie machten. As in the present tense, stems ending in -d/-t insert an -e- (ich arbeitete, du arbeitetest), and stems in -m/-n + consonant often do too (ich öffnete, ich regnete). If you can conjugate the present tense, the weak Präteritum is one small step: swap the present endings for the -te set.

Strong verbs: vowel change, no -te

Strong verbs change their stem vowel and take no -te — and the ich and er/sie/es forms take no ending at all: ich fuhr, du fuhrst, er fuhr, wir fuhren, ihr fuhrt, sie fuhren. This bare-stem shape (ich kam, er sah, ich blieb, er schrieb) is the visual signature of the strong past. The vowel changes fall into families worth learning as groups: a→u (fahren→fuhr, tragen→trug, schlagen→schlug, graben→grub, waschen→wusch), e→a (sprechen→sprach, sehen→sah, lesen→las, essen→aß, geben→gab, nehmen→nahm, helfen→half, treffen→traf), i→a→u (singen→sang, trinken→trank, finden→fand, beginnen→begann, gewinnen→gewann, schwimmen→schwamm), ei→ie (bleiben→blieb, schreiben→schrieb, steigen→stieg, scheinen→schien), ie→o (fliegen→flog, ziehen→zog, frieren→fror, verlieren→verlor, biegen→bog). Learn them in these families and the "irregular" verbs start looking systematic.

Mixed verbs: the in-betweeners

Seven common verbs change their vowel and take the weak -te endings — the mixed class: bringen→brachte, denken→dachte, wissen→wusste, kennen→kannte, nennen→nannte, rennen→rannte, brennen→brannte, plus haben→hatte (the most frequent of all). So: ich brachte, du brachtest, er brachte; ich wusste, du wusstest. These are high-frequency verbs — wissen, kennen, denken, bringen appear constantly — so they repay direct memorisation early. Note senden and wenden, which accept both forms (sandte/sendete).

Präteritum vs. Perfekt: when to use which

This is the question every learner asks, and the answer is about register, not meaning. In spoken German, the Perfekt rules: ich habe gemacht, ich bin gegangen. The Präteritum in speech survives mainly for sein, haben, werden and the modals — ich war, ich hatte, ich wurde, ich konnte, ich musste, ich wollte — which sound more natural than their Perfekt forms in conversation. In written German (novels, news, reports, formal emails), the Präteritum is the default narrative tense: Er ging zur Tür und öffnete sie. Exams test both, but reading comprehension lives or dies on recognising strong Präteritum forms — which is exactly what the conjugator above trains.

Word order: the verb still goes second

The Präteritum changes nothing about sentence structure: the conjugated verb still occupies position two in main clauses — Gestern fuhr er nach Berlin — and moves to the end in subordinate clauses: …, weil er nach Berlin fuhr. One form, one position, same as the present tense. This is why teachers introduce the Präteritum early despite its "past" label: grammatically it's the simplest past tense German has.

Modal verbs in the Präteritum: the spoken exception

The modal verbs deserve their own table because they are the Präteritum forms you'll actually speak. Each modal loses its umlaut and takes weak -te endings: können→ich konnte, müssen→ich musste, dürfen→ich durfte, sollen→ich sollte, wollen→ich wollte, mögen→ich mochte. Full paradigm for können: ich konnte, du konntest, er konnte, wir konnten, ihr konntet, sie konnten. In conversation, ich konnte nicht kommen sounds far more natural than the Perfekt ich habe nicht kommen können (with its infamous double infinitive). The same applies to wissen (ich wusste) and the full set above. Practical rule: for modals, sein, haben and werden, default to the Präteritum even when speaking; for every other verb, default to the Perfekt in speech and the Präteritum in writing. This one habit instantly makes your spoken German sound more native.

Regional colour: the North-South split

A quirk worth knowing: in southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland, the Präteritum has nearly vanished from speech entirely — even ich war often becomes ich bin gewesen in casual Bavarian or Austrian conversation, and written Southern German still uses it normally. In the north, the spoken Präteritum of sein/haben/modals is standard. As a learner this changes nothing about what you should produce — the "modals + sein/haben/were in Präteritum when speaking" rule works everywhere — but it explains why you'll hear ich bin gewesen from a Munich taxi driver and ich war from a Hamburg one. Both are correct; they're just different regional defaults layered over the same written standard.

How to practise

Conjugate ten verbs above — five weak, five strong — saying each table aloud before checking. Then convert: take five present-tense sentences from your own routine and rewrite them as yesterday's news (Ich stehe auf → Ich stand auf; Ich esse → Ich aß). Finally, read one short news paragraph and circle every Präteritum form, naming weak/strong/mixed for each. Recognition first, production second — the strong forms in particular need to be seen dozens of times before they're produced correctly.

Frequently asked questions

How do you conjugate machen in the Präteritum?

ich machte, du machtest, er machte, wir machten, ihr machtet, sie machten. Stem + -te + the endings -∅, -st, -∅, -n, -t, -n.

How do you conjugate sein in the Präteritum?

ich war, du warst, er war, wir waren, ihr wart, sie waren. Strong verb: stem changes to war-, no -te, no ending on ich/er.

When do Germans use Präteritum vs. Perfekt?

Perfekt dominates conversation; Präteritum dominates writing. In speech, the Präteritum survives mainly for sein, haben, werden and modals (ich war, ich konnte).

What are mixed verbs?

Verbs with a vowel change plus weak -te endings: bringen→brachte, denken→dachte, wissen→wusste, kennen→kannte, haben→hatte.

Why is there no ending on "ich fuhr"?

Strong Präteritum ich and er/sie/es forms take no ending: ich fuhr, er kam, ich sah. The bare changed stem is the signature of the strong past.
