What Is the Nominative Case?

Every German sentence has a subject — the person or thing that performs the action. The grammatical case used for that subject is the nominative case (Nominativ). It is the first of the four German cases and the one every learner meets first. It is also the “dictionary form” of every noun: when you look up a German word, the article shown with it — der, die, or das — is its nominative article. That is why learning the article together with each new noun, right from the start, is the single most important habit in German.

The nominative answers the questions wer? (who?) and was? (what?) about the verb. In the sentence “Der Mann liest ein Buch” (the man reads a book), asking “Who reads?” gives you “der Mann” — and that tells you the subject is in the nominative. Because word order in German is flexible, identifying the nominative article is often the only way to know who is doing what: “Der Hund beißt den Mann” and “Den Mann beißt der Hund” both mean the dog bites the man, because only the dog carries the nominative article.

When Do You Use the Nominative?

There are exactly two jobs for the nominative case, and once you know them you will recognise it everywhere:

  1. The subject of the sentence. Whoever or whatever performs the verb takes the nominative. “Die Katze schläft” (the cat sleeps) — die Katze is doing the sleeping, so it is nominative. “Das Wasser ist kalt” (the water is cold) — das Wasser is the subject being described.
  2. Predicate nouns after sein, werden, and bleiben. After the verbs “to be”, “to become”, and “to remain”, the noun that renames the subject also stands in the nominative — it is not an object. “Er ist ein Lehrer” (he is a teacher), “Sie wird Ärztin” (she is becoming a doctor), “Wir bleiben Freunde” (we remain friends). This surprises English speakers who expect a different case after “is”, but in German both sides of “sein” are nominative.

Everything else — the direct object, the indirect object, anything after most prepositions — belongs to the accusative, dative, or genitive. But those cases only make sense once the nominative is automatic, which is exactly what the drill above trains.

Two things to notice. First, the plural always takes die in the nominative, no matter what gender the noun has in the singular: der Mann → die Männer, die Frau → die Frauen, das Kind → die Kinder. Second, there is no indefinite article in the plural — you simply say “Kinder spielen” (children play), not “eine Kinder”.

How to Guess Noun Gender

German noun gender feels random at first, but many endings follow reliable patterns. Use these as shortcuts — and still learn the article with every new word:

  • Usually masculine (der): days, months, seasons, and directions (der Montag, der Januar, der Sommer, der Norden); most weather nouns (der Regen, der Schnee, der Wind); nouns ending in -er that describe people (der Lehrer, der Fahrer); many nouns ending in -el or -en (der Apfel, der Garten).
  • Usually feminine (die): nouns ending in -ung (die Zeitung, die Übung), -heit (die Gesundheit), -keit (die Möglichkeit), -schaft (die Freundschaft), -tät (die Universität), -ion (die Nation), -ik (die Musik), and -ei (die Bäckerei); female persons and professions (die Lehrerin, die Ärztin); most nouns ending in -e (die Katze, die Tür, die Lampe).
  • Usually neuter (das): diminutives ending in -chen or -lein — always neuter, even for people and animals (das Mädchen, das Kätzchen); nouns ending in -ment (das Instrument); most nouns beginning with Ge- (das Gebäude, das Gespräch); metals and chemical elements (das Gold, das Eisen); young animals and babies (das Kind, das Kalb).

These rules cover a large share of everyday vocabulary, but exceptions exist (der Löffel, die Gabel, das Messer — the famous cutlery trio every learner memorises). When in doubt, trust your dictionary over your guess.

___ Tür ist offen. → Die Tür ist offen.
“Tür” ends in -e, a strong feminine signal → die. English: “The door is open.”
___ Fenster sind geschlossen. → Die Fenster sind geschlossen.
Singular das Fenster is neuter, but this is plural — and plural always takes die. English: “The windows are closed.”

Common Learner Mistakes

  • Guessing gender instead of learning it. Saying “das Tür” or “der Wasser” marks you as a beginner instantly. Always learn nouns as der/die/das + noun — flashcards should never show a bare noun.
  • Using the accusative einen for a masculine subject. “Einen Mann ist hier” is wrong; the subject needs the nominative “Ein Mann ist hier”. Remember: einen only appears when the masculine noun is the direct object (“Ich sehe einen Mann” — I see a man).
  • Forgetting that plural is always die. Learners who know “das Kind” sometimes write “das Kinder”. The singular gender disappears in the plural — it is always die Kinder, die Männer, die Fenster.
  • Putting a predicate noun in the accusative. “Er ist einen Lehrer” is a classic error. After sein, werden, and bleiben the noun stays nominative: “Er ist ein Lehrer”.
  • Forgetting capitalisation. All German nouns are capitalised — Mann, Frau, Kind. This is not decoration; it helps you spot nouns (and therefore subjects) while reading.

How to Use This Drill

Read each sentence, find the subject, and choose the nominative article that fits its gender — der for masculine, die for feminine, das for neuter, and die again for plurals. You will get instant green or red feedback, the English translation, and a short gender note for every sentence. Aim for 13 out of 15 or better, then repeat the drill the next day: spaced repetition is what moves article choice from conscious effort to reflex. Once the nominative feels automatic, you are ready for the accusative — where der becomes den and the real fun begins.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the nominative case in German?

The nominative case (Nominativ) is the case of the subject — the person or thing performing the action of the verb. It is also the dictionary form of every noun, so the article you learn with a new word (der, die, das) is its nominative article.

When do you use the nominative case?

Use the nominative for the subject of the sentence (“Der Mann liest”) and for predicate nouns after sein, werden, and bleiben (“Sie ist eine Lehrerin” — she is a teacher). These are its only two jobs.

What are the nominative articles?

Der (masculine), die (feminine), das (neuter), and die for the plural of all genders. The indefinite articles are ein (masculine/neuter), eine (feminine), with no indefinite article in the plural.

Is it “ein Mann” or “einen Mann” as the subject?

“Ein Mann ist hier” is correct. Einen is the accusative form and only appears when the masculine noun is the direct object, as in “Ich sehe einen Mann” (I see a man).

How can I remember whether a noun is der, die, or das?

Learn the article with every new noun from day one, and lean on ending patterns: -ung/-heit/-keit/-schaft/-tät are nearly always feminine (die), -chen/-lein diminutives are always neuter (das), and days, months, seasons, and weather nouns are usually masculine (der).

Why is the plural always “die”?

In the nominative, plural nouns take die regardless of their singular gender — der Mann → die Männer, das Kind → die Kinder. Gender distinctions simply collapse in the plural, which is one small mercy of German grammar.
