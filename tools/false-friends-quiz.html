False friends: the words that lie to your face

German and English are cousins, and cousins share family resemblances — including the misleading kind. A false friend is a word that looks or sounds like an English word but means something different, sometimes dangerously different. Das Gift is not a present; it is poison. Bekommen does not mean "to become"; it means "to receive". Brav does not mean brave; it means well-behaved — so praising a child as brav says nothing about courage. This tool collects the 40 false friends that actually ambush English-speaking learners, each with the trap meaning, the true meaning, and a real example sentence.

The quiz is engineered around the trap: the false meaning is always one of the four options. That is deliberate cruelty with a pedagogical purpose. In real life the trap meaning is always present in your head — your English brain offers it for free, instantly, confidently. The only way to beat it is to meet each trap explicitly, get burned by it in a safe quiz, and attach a warning label to the word. Every feedback message names the trap out loud, because a trap you have named is a trap you can dodge.

Why German–English has so many of them

Three historical forces manufacture false friends. First, shared Germanic roots that drifted apart: hell (bright) and English "hell", bald (soon) and English "bald", fast (almost) and English "fast" — same ancestors, different life choices. Second, Latin and French loans that each language borrowed separately: aktuell (current) vs "actual", eventuell (possibly) vs "eventually", sensibel (sensitive) vs "sensible", sympathisch (likeable) vs "sympathetic". Third — and most entertaining — English words that German borrowed and reassigned: das Handy (mobile phone), der Beamer (projector), der Oldtimer (vintage car), das Public Viewing (public screening), das Shooting (photo shoot), der Body (bodysuit). Germans use these confidently, assuming they mean the same in English. They do not.

The danger list: ten you must know cold

Some false friends are funny; some cause real damage. Das Gift (poison) on a warning sign is the famous one — misread it and the consequences are literal. Bekommen vs "become" corrupts everyday sentences: Ich bekomme ein Baby means "I am having/receiving a baby" in the sense of getting one, not turning into one. Der Chef is your boss, not the cook — asking to "speak to the Chef" in a restaurant requests the manager. Die Kaution is your rental deposit, not a caution; confusing it with die Rente (pension) vs die Miete (rent) turns apartment hunting into a minefield. Das Rezept is a recipe or a prescription — showing a pharmacist your "receipt" (die Quittung) gets you nowhere. Der Termin is an appointment: Ich habe einen Termin never means "I have a term". Die Note is a school grade, so eine gute Note is praise, not stationery. Sich blamieren is to embarrass yourself — nobody is being blamed. And der Mist: it means manure or rubbish, and as an exclamation (So ein Mist!) it is mild profanity, roughly "crap!" — while actual fog is der Nebel.

Denglisch: English words wearing German costumes

The fourth category in this tool deserves its own warning. Modern German absorbed hundreds of English words and gave many of them new jobs. Der Beamer projects slides; a BMW is just a BMW. Das Public Viewing is a joyful open-air World Cup screening — telling an English speaker you are "going to a public viewing" suggests a funeral. Der Smoking is a tuxedo (from "smoking jacket", the jacket then the whole suit). Der Oldtimer is a classic car, never a person. Das Handy — the crown jewel — comes from a brand-adjacent coinage and is now the universal German word for mobile phone; handy the adjective barely exists in German. These are not learner errors; they are real German. You must learn them as German words that happen to look English, full stop.

How to make false friends harmless

The standard advice — "just learn the real meaning" — fails because the trap meaning is automatic and the real meaning is effortful. The method that works is explicit trap labelling: for each word, learn a triple — the word, the trap, the truth. Gift — NOT gift — poison. Bekommen — NOT become — receive. Say the triple aloud; the "NOT" is the active ingredient. This tool's browse view shows every card as exactly that triple, and the summary at the end of a failed quiz lists your personal trap set in the same format. Drill that list, not the whole bank.

Second technique: sentence anchoring. A false friend learned in isolation stays dangerous; one learned inside a vivid sentence becomes safe. Das Gift ist tödlich (the poison is deadly) makes "gift = poison" unforgettable in a way no flashcard can. Every entry here carries an example for exactly this reason — steal the sentences, say them aloud, and attach the emotion. Third: in the wild, when a German sentence makes perfect English sense word-by-word but the situation feels off, suspect a false friend before suspecting your comprehension. Nine times out of ten, the word is lying to you, not the sentence.

Frequently asked questions

What is a false friend in German?

A German word that looks like an English word but means something different — e.g. das Gift means poison (not gift), bekommen means to receive (not to become), and brav means well-behaved (not brave).

What does 'das Gift' mean in German?

Poison — never a present. It is the most famous German-English false friend, and misreading it on a warning sign has literal consequences.

What does 'Handy' mean in German?

Mobile phone. Das Handy is the universal German word for a cell phone; the English adjective 'handy' (useful) barely exists in German.

What does 'bekommen' mean?

To receive or to get — Ich habe ein Geschenk bekommen means 'I received a present'. It never means 'to become'; that is werden.

Why do Germans say 'Public Viewing' and 'Beamer'?

German borrowed these English words and reassigned them: das Public Viewing is a public event screening (e.g. World Cup), der Beamer is a projector. They are real German words that happen to look English.

How do I stop falling for false friends?

Learn each word as a triple — word, trap, truth (Gift — NOT gift — poison) — and anchor it in a vivid example sentence. Drill your personal trap list, not the whole bank.
