Relative clauses: the pronoun table that unlocks German sentences

A relative clause describes a noun more closely: Der Mann, der dort steht, ist mein Vater. (The man who is standing there is my father.) The relative pronoun (der/die/das …) does two jobs at once: it agrees with the noun it refers to in gender and number, and it takes its case from its role inside the relative clause — not from the main clause. That second job is where learners stumble: in Ich kenne den Mann, dem ich geholfen habe, the main clause has accusative den Mann, but the pronoun is dative dem because inside the relative clause it is the indirect object of helfen.

The new territory is dative and genitive — dem/der/denen and dessen/deren. Note denen: the dative plural relative pronoun is the only form in the whole table with an -n ending, and examiners love testing exactly that.

Verb-final word order

The conjugated verb of a relative clause goes to the end, like every subordinate clause: Die Frau, die ich gestern gesehen habe, … Das Buch, das auf dem Tisch liegt, … Die Kinder, die im Garten spielen, … This is the same verb-final rule as dass- and weil-clauses — one rule, many clauses. The comma before the pronoun is mandatory, and a second comma closes the clause before the main verb resumes: Der Mann, der dort steht, lacht.

Prepositions go before the pronoun

When the relative pronoun follows a preposition, the preposition stands before it and determines its case: Das Haus, in dem ich wohne, ist alt. (in + dative) Die Stadt, aus der er kommt, ist klein. (aus + dative) Der Film, über den wir gesprochen haben, war gut. (über + accusative) Die Freundin, mit der ich lerne, ist nett. (mit + dative) The stranded preposition of English ("the house I live in") does not exist in standard German — the preposition must precede the pronoun. (Colloquial wo — das Haus, wo ich wohne — is dialectal; avoid it in writing and exams.)

Dessen and deren: the genitive pronouns

The genitive relative pronouns express possession: Der Student, dessen Vater Arzt ist, … (whose father) Die Frau, deren Auto kaputt ist, … Die Leute, deren Kinder hier spielen, … Two forms only: dessen for masculine/neuter, deren for feminine/plural. The pronoun sits directly before the possessed noun with no article: dessen Vater, never dessen der Vater. In formal writing you will also meet the preposition-split variant Der Mann, dessen wegen ich kam (rare; colloquial prefers wegen dem).

Was, wer, and the special cases

After indefinite antecedents — alles, nichts, etwas, das — and after superlatives, German uses was, not das: Alles, was du sagst, ist wahr. Das ist das Beste, was ich je gegessen habe. Nichts, was er tut, hilft. Free relative clauses (no antecedent noun at all) use wer/wen/wem: Wer das sagt, lügt. Wem du vertraust, ist deine Sache. These are the two exceptions to the der/die/das table — everything else with a concrete noun antecedent uses the table.

Common mistakes

  • Copying the main clause's case: Ich kenne den Mann, den ich geholfen habe — wrong; helfen takes dative: dem ich geholfen habe.
  • Dative plural: Die Leute, denen ich geholfen habe — the pronoun is denen, not den.
  • Missing commas: relative clauses are always comma-wrapped in German, even short ones.
  • Stranding the preposition: Das Haus, das ich in wohne — wrong; Das Haus, in dem ich wohne.
  • Using was after a concrete noun: Der Mann, was dort steht — wrong; der. Was is only for alles/nichts/etwas/das and superlatives.
  • Forgetting verb-final: Der Mann, der steht dort — wrong; der dort steht.

Relative clauses in formal and medical German

In the Fachsprachenprüfung, relative clauses are a register marker: precise documentation runs on them. Der Patient, der über thorakale Schmerzen klagt, wurde stationär aufgenommen. Die Medikation, die bisher verabreicht wurde, zeigte keine Wirkung. Examiners read your Arztbrief for exactly this kind of compact, correct subordination — a missing comma or a wrong pronoun case reads as carelessness. The same applies to academic German: definitions, citations, and method descriptions all lean on relative clauses (Die Studie, deren Ergebnisse 2024 publiziert wurden, …). Mastering the table is therefore not decorative grammar; it is the syntax of professional credibility.

Frequently asked questions

How do you choose the correct German relative pronoun?

Match gender and number to the noun it refers to, then take the case from the pronoun's role INSIDE the relative clause. Example: Ich kenne den Mann, dem ich geholfen habe — dem is dative because helfen takes dative, even though the main clause has den Mann.

What are the dative and genitive relative pronouns?

Dative: dem (masc/neut), der (fem), denen (plural). Genitive: dessen (masc/neut), deren (fem/plural). Denen is the only relative pronoun with an -n ending.

Where does the verb go in a German relative clause?

At the end, like all subordinate clauses: Die Frau, die ich gestern gesehen habe, … The clause is always wrapped in commas.

Can a preposition come after the relative pronoun?

No — in standard German the preposition must precede the pronoun and determines its case: Das Haus, in dem ich wohne (not 'das ich in wohne'). Colloquial 'wo' (das Haus, wo ich wohne) is dialectal.

When do you use 'was' instead of 'der/die/das'?

After alles, nichts, etwas, das, and superlatives: Alles, was du sagst …; Das ist das Beste, was ich je gegessen habe. Free relative clauses without a noun use wer/wen/wem: Wer das sagt, lügt.
