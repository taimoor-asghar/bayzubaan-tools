Why medical German is its own subject

Everyday German gets you through a bakery. It does not get you through a ward round. Medical German is a specialised register with three features that make it worth studying on its own: a large Latin- and Greek-derived vocabulary that often differs from the everyday word (you say die Bauchspeicheldrüse to a patient but write Pankreas in the chart), a set of fixed clinical phrases (Die Anamnese ist unauffällig, Der Befund ist unauffällig) that native doctors produce without thinking, and a documentation style in the passive, nominalised register (Es wurde eine Blutuntersuchung durchgeführt rather than wir haben Blut abgenommen).

This deck targets exactly the vocabulary tested in the Fachsprachenprüfung (FSP) — the medical German exam foreign doctors must pass to practise in Germany. The FSP has three parts: a doctor–patient conversation (Anamnese), a written documentation task, and a doctor–doctor handover. All three run on the vocabulary in this deck: symptoms for the conversation, anatomy for the examination, procedures and treatment terms for the documentation and handover. Knowing these 60 cards cold removes a large share of the exam's lexical pressure.

The two registers: patient language vs. chart language

The single most important skill the deck trains implicitly is register switching. To the patient you say die Schmerzen and die Übelkeit; in the documentation you write Schmerzsymptomatik and Nausea. To the patient: das Herzrasen; in the handover: Palpitationen or Tachykardie. Patients say der Durchfall; the chart says Diarrhö. The FSP examiners explicitly watch for this: using Latin terms with a simulated patient counts against you, and using colloquial terms in the documentation does too.

A practical rule: with patients, use the everyday German term and short sentences; with colleagues, use the precise term and the nominal style. The example sentences on the backs of these cards are written in the patient-facing register — Haben Sie Übelkeit?, Wo sind die Schmerzen am stärksten? — because that is the register most learners under-practise. Drill them aloud: the FSP conversation part is spoken, and phrases you have only read silently will not come out fluently under exam pressure.

Anatomy: articles matter

Notice that every anatomy card carries its article: der Kopf, die Lunge, das Herz. In a clinical setting you cannot afford to hesitate over der vs. die — Die Leber ist vergrößert has to come out correctly the first time, because case errors in the handover undermine your credibility with the examiners. If articles are still shaky, run the german-article-trainer tool first, then return here: the medical terms are just nouns, and the article system works the same way.

Two anatomy traps: die Blase means bladder (not "blister" — that is die Blase too, actually, so context decides), and der Darm covers both small and large intestine in everyday clinical speech; der Dickdarm and der Dünndarm specify. Die Milz and die Bauchspeicheldrüse are long but high-yield: spleen and pancreas come up constantly in abdominal examinations, and mispronouncing them is a classic giveaway of an unprepared candidate.

Clinical phrases that do the heavy lifting

A handful of sentence frames cover most of the FSP conversation. Memorise them as wholes: Seit wann haben Sie …? (since when), Wo sind die Schmerzen am stärksten?, Strahlen die Schmerzen aus? (do they radiate), Haben Sie … bemerkt?, Leiden Sie unter …?, Gibt es Vorerkrankungen?, Nehmen Sie regelmäßig Medikamente? (do you take medication regularly — not in this deck, but pair it with die Dosis and die Tablette). With these frames plus the symptom cards, you can conduct a complete pain history in German.

For the documentation part, the workhorse frames are: Die Anamnese ergab …, Bei der Untersuchung zeigte sich …, Die Laborwerte waren …, Es wurde … veranlasst (was ordered), Die Entlassung ist für … geplant. And for the handover, the classic opening: Ich übergebe Herrn/Frau X, … Jahre alt, aufgenommen wegen … — followed by der Befund, die Diagnose, and the treatment plan in the nominal style. The deck's Behandlung category feeds directly into these.

How to study this deck

Work one category at a time — Symptome first, since the patient conversation is the part most candidates fear most. Click to flip, say the English meaning aloud before flipping (recognition without recall is not learning), then mark honestly. Cards you mark "still learning" accumulate in the review pile; that pile is your next session. Do not move to the next category until the pile is empty two sessions in a row.

Then reverse the direction yourself: look at the English meaning and produce the German term with its article. The deck shows German first, but the exam runs both ways — the patient describes symptoms in German and you must understand, then you must produce German questions. Finally, chain cards into a mini-consultation: open with Welche Beschwerden haben Sie?, follow the patient's (imagined) answer with Seit wann?, Wo genau?, and close with Wir machen eine Blutuntersuchung. Five minutes of this daily beats an hour of silent card-flipping.

A note on accuracy

This deck was authored with clinical precision in mind: every German term is the standard term used in German hospitals, and every English gloss is the term an English-speaking doctor would use. Where a word has two clinical senses (die Blase), the example sentence disambiguates. That said, medicine evolves and regional usage varies — if your German medical mentor uses a different term for something here, learn theirs alongside. The exam rewards the term your examiners expect, and this deck covers the consensus core that every examiner accepts.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Fachsprachenprüfung?

The FSP is the medical German exam that foreign-trained doctors must pass to obtain the Berufserlaubnis (temporary licence) in Germany. It tests doctor–patient conversation, written documentation, and a doctor–doctor handover — all in German, at roughly C1 level with medical vocabulary.

Should I learn Latin terms or everyday German words?

Both, in the right register: everyday German with patients (die Schmerzen, die Übelkeit), Latin/Greek terms with colleagues and in documentation (Schmerzsymptomatik, Nausea). Mixing them up — Latin with patients, colloquial in the chart — costs marks in the FSP.

How many medical terms do I need for the FSP?

There is no official list, but the working core is a few hundred terms: symptoms, anatomy, common diagnoses, procedures, and medication vocabulary. These 65 cards cover the highest-frequency core; they are the foundation, not the ceiling.

Why do all the anatomy cards include the article?

Because in clinical German you produce noun phrases constantly — die Leber ist vergrößert, der Befund ist unauffällig — and hesitating over der/die/das undermines fluency. Learn every medical noun with its article from day one.

How is this different from a normal vocabulary deck?

The example sentences are clinical, not everyday: they model the exact phrases used in anamnesis, examination, and handover. You are learning sentence frames and register, not just word pairs.

Can I use this deck if I am not a doctor?

Yes — the vocabulary is useful for nurses, medical students, and anyone who might need to describe symptoms to a German-speaking doctor. The FSP-specific guidance targets doctors, but the words are the same.
