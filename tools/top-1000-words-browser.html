Why the most frequent words matter most

Vocabulary is the single biggest predictor of how well you understand real German. But not all words are equal: the 1,000 most frequent German words cover roughly 85% of everyday texts, from newspaper articles to conversations with colleagues. That means the first hundred words you learn do far more work than the next thousand. This tool focuses on the highest-frequency core — the words you will meet on day one and keep meeting for years.

Frequency lists are built by counting words across large text corpora (millions of words from newspapers, books and spoken language). The ranking below follows standard German frequency data: articles, pronouns, prepositions and the core modal and auxiliary verbs sit at the top, because they hold every sentence together. Content words — nouns, main verbs, adjectives — follow in order of their real-world frequency, not in textbook-chapter order.

Smart learning tips for German vocabulary

First, learn verbs with their prepositions and cases: not just denken, but denken an + Akkusativ (to think of). The example sentences here model the most common patterns, but a good habit is to note the case after each preposition you meet. Second, learn adjectives with their opposites: teuer/billig, leicht/schwer, groß/klein — pairs stick together in memory and double your coverage for free.

Third, pay special attention to the "false friends" trap hiding in plain sight: werden looks like "were" but means "to become"; bald means "soon", not "bald"; Gift (not in this list, but coming) means "poison". When a word feels suspiciously familiar, double-check it. Fourth, notice the verbs with stem changes — sprechen (du sprichst), nehmen (du nimmst), sehen (du siehst) — and learn the du and er/sie/es forms from the start, not as an afterthought.

Notice the word order in every example sentence

Every example sentence in this tool is also a quiet grammar lesson. Look at the main clauses first: the conjugated verb sits in second position, no matter what comes first. Ich weiß die Antwort. starts with the subject, but Morgen komme ich. (the same pattern) starts with a time expression and the verb still comes second — komme follows morgen immediately. Get used to this now: in German, position two belongs to the verb.

Now compare the two "because" sentences in the bank. Ich bleibe, denn ich bin müde keeps normal word order after denn, because denn is a coordinating conjunction joining two main clauses. But Ich bleibe, weil ich müde bin sends the verb bin to the very end — weil is subordinating and pushes the conjugated verb last. This denn/weil contrast is one of the most tested distinctions in German exams, and it appears here in minimal, memorable pairs. Read both sentences aloud until the pattern feels automatic.

Separable verbs give you a third pattern for free: Bring bitte Brot mit splits mitbringen, parking the prefix at the end. And modal verbs park the infinitive at the end: Ich kann Deutsch sprechen, Du sollst mehr Wasser trinken. Once you see these frames — verb second, verb last after weil/wenn/dass, prefix or infinitive at the end — hundreds of sentences become predictable. That is why every card here shows a full sentence: you are learning the word and its frame together.

Frequently asked questions

Why learn the most frequent words first?

The top 1,000 German words cover roughly 85% of everyday texts. Learning by frequency gives you the best return on every minute you study — the first 100 words unlock far more understanding than the next 1,000 rare ones.

Should I really learn every noun with its article?

Yes — always. A noun without der/die/das is only half learned: the article determines adjective endings, pronouns and prepositions throughout the sentence. The plural ending matters too, which is why every noun card here shows both.

How does the flashcard test work?

It shows the German word first. Say the English meaning out loud, tap to reveal the answer, then mark it known or missed. Missed words are re-queued automatically, so you see them twice — a simple spaced-repetition loop.

How many new words should I learn per day?

Five to ten, with daily review of yesterday's batch. Spaced, repeated retrieval beats cramming: ten minutes a day outperforms an hour once a week. Come back tomorrow and run the deck again.

What is the difference between wissen and kennen?

Wissen is knowing facts ('Ich weiß die Antwort'); kennen is knowing people or places through familiarity ('Ich kenne Berlin'). Both rank in the top 1,000 and both are tested constantly in exams.

Do example sentences really help vocabulary stick?

Yes — context fixes the meaning and shows you the word's real grammar: its preposition, its case, its word order. A word learned in a sentence is remembered far longer than a word learned from a list.
