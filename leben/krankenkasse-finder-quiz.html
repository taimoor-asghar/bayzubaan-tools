How the German system works: two systems, one obligation

Since 2009, every person resident in Germany must have health insurance — you cannot opt out, and you cannot stay uninsured. The system has two branches. Public health insurance (GKV, gesetzliche Krankenversicherung) covers about 90% of residents: contributions are a percentage of income, the medical package is defined by law and is around 95% identical across all funds, pre-existing conditions can never be refused, and spouses and children can be insured for free (Familienversicherung). Private health insurance (PKV) instead prices on age, health and chosen tariff, insures each person separately, and offers tariff-dependent extras — but coming back to the public system later is difficult, and after 55 virtually impossible.

Which branch you may enter is not a matter of taste for most newcomers: the law decides it from your employment status, age and income. That is what the quiz above maps for you.

Public or private? An honest comparison (no sales pitch)

GKV strengths: price follows income, not health; family members insured free; no medical underwriting; you can switch funds every 12 months; benefits are largely identical everywhere, so the choice is mostly about service and price. GKV weaknesses: high earners pay more than they might privately; waiting times and coverage ceilings are the same for everyone.

PKV strengths: potentially cheaper when you are young, healthy and high-earning; tariff choice (single room, chief physician, broader dental); sometimes faster specialist appointments. PKV weaknesses: premiums rise with age regardless of income; every family member needs their own contract; and the exit back to GKV is narrow — if your income later falls below the threshold you may return, but from 55 on it is effectively closed. Expat guides put it bluntly: treat PKV as close to a one-way door.

Where students, freelancers and job seekers land

Students under 25 can usually stay on a parent's GKV policy for free (Familienversicherung) — check whether the parent is publicly insured. Ages 25–30 (within 14 semesters) get the cheap student rate (KVdS), around €141–€146/month all-in with TK. Over 30, students fall into voluntary GKV — roughly €274/month all-in at current published fund figures, varying by fund.

Freelancers and the self-employed have free choice: voluntary GKV (income-based, with a statutory minimum assessment base — ask the fund for the current figure before signing) or PKV. Note that freelancers pay the full contribution themselves; there is no employer half.

Job seekers and the newly arrived still need cover from day one. A short-term incoming/travel policy can bridge the gap until employment starts — but non-EU newcomers should know the Ausländeramt (foreigners' office) may not accept thin travel cover as sufficient for a residence permit; a proper German policy is the safe route.

Non-working spouses and children of a GKV member are covered free under Familienversicherung — one of the strongest arguments for public insurance for families. If the working spouse is privately insured, family members need their own cover instead.

EU/EEA citizens posted temporarily (with an S1 form) or visiting can use their EHIC for medically necessary care — but the EHIC is a travel document, not residence insurance. Once you register your address (Anmeldung) and live in Germany, you join the German system like everyone else.

How switching between public funds works

You are not locked in forever: after 12 months of membership you can switch GKV funds freely. The new fund handles the cancellation with the old one, so coverage never gaps — and you have 14 days from your job start to tell your employer which fund you chose (otherwise the employer picks one for you). The quiz verdict is a starting point, not a marriage.

Frequently asked questions

Is health insurance really compulsory in Germany?

Yes — everyone resident in Germany must be insured, publicly or privately. Newcomers arrange cover before or right after arrival; employer, university and registration office all ask for proof.

I earn under €6,450 a month as an employee — do I have a choice?

No. At or below the 2026 threshold of €77,400/year you are in public insurance by law. You do get to choose which public fund — TK, AOK, Barmer or any other.

How much does public health insurance cost in 2026?

14.6% plus the fund's supplementary contribution (average ~2.9%): TK 17.29%, Barmer 17.89%, DAK 17.80% total, calculated on income up to €5,812.50/month — and employees pay only half. Students pay a flat €110.38 plus long-term care.

Can I switch from private back to public insurance later?

Rarely. Returning requires becoming compulsorily insured again (e.g. salary under the threshold), and after 55 it is virtually impossible. Treat private insurance as close to a one-way door.

I'm an EU citizen with an EHIC — is that enough to live in Germany?

No. The EHIC covers medically necessary care during temporary stays — it is not residence insurance. Once you live and register in Germany, you join the German system like everyone else.

TK, AOK or Barmer — which should a newcomer pick?

All three run English-language service (verified on their official pages). Rates differ slightly for 2026, but with ~95% of benefits identical by law, English support and service usually matter more than the small price gap. No affiliate links, no paid rankings on this page.
