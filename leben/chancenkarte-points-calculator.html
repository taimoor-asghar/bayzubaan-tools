What the Chancenkarte is

The Chancenkarte (Opportunity Card) is Germany's points-based residence permit for skilled workers from outside the EU, introduced in June 2024 under sections 20a and 20b of the Residence Act (AufenthG). It lets you live in Germany for up to one year while you look for qualified employment — without needing a job offer first. While searching, you may work in trial jobs and part-time work to support yourself.

The core idea is simple: instead of proving a job offer up front, you prove potential through a transparent points grid. Reach 6 points, meet the three baseline requirements, and you qualify. If your foreign professional qualification is fully recognised in Germany, you skip the grid entirely — recognition alone makes you eligible.

The baseline requirements (non-negotiable)

Before counting a single point, three things must hold:

  1. A recognised qualification. Your vocational or academic qualification must be recognised in Germany, or at least partially recognised (partial recognition itself earns 4 points, which is why it carries the most weight).
  2. Minimum language skills. At least German A1 or English B2, certified. Without one of these, the points do not help you.
  3. Proof of funds. You must show you can support yourself during the job search — roughly €1,091 per month, typically through a blocked account (Sperrkonto).

Two rules catch people out. First, German levels don't stack — B2 gives 3 points total, not 1+2+3. Second, experience doesn't stack either — five years gives 3, not 2+3. The calculator above applies both rules automatically.

How to read your result

6 or more points with all three baselines ticked means you meet the published criteria — the realistic next step is the official self-check on make-it-in-germany.com and then the actual application at the German mission in your country. Below 6, the planner lists the points you haven't claimed yet, ordered from easiest to hardest, so you can see the cheapest route across the line. Common closers: a certified English C1 (+1), moving German from A2 to B1 (+1), or checking whether your occupation is actually on the shortage list (+1).

Worked examples

A nurse, 32, from the Philippines. Partial recognition (+4), nursing is a shortage occupation (+1), 3 years' experience (+2), German B1 (+2), English C1 (+1), age 32 (+2). Total: 12 points — qualifies comfortably.

A software engineer, 29, from India. Partial recognition (+4), IT on the shortage list (+1), 2 years' experience (+2), German A2 (+1), English C1 (+1), age 29 (+2). Total: 11 points — qualifies.

An electrician, 39, from Brazil. Qualification not yet recognised (+0), electrical work on the shortage list (+1), 2 years' experience (+2), German A2 (+1), English B2 (+0), age 39 (+1). Total: 5 points — one short. The cheapest fixes: certify English C1 (+1), reach German B1 (+1), or start the recognition process (+4).

Notice what the examples share: the grid treats every nationality identically. Points come from what you can document — qualification, experience, languages — never from where you were born.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Chancenkarte (Opportunity Card)?

Germany's points-based residence permit for non-EU skilled workers, introduced in June 2024 under sections 20a/20b of the Residence Act. It lets you live in Germany for up to a year to find qualified work, without a job offer in hand.

How many points do I need for the Chancenkarte?

At least 6, from the official grid: partial recognition (4), shortage occupation (1), experience (2 or 3), German level (1–3), English C1 (1 extra), age (2 or 1), previous stay (1), qualifying spouse (1). Full recognition of your qualification means automatic eligibility with no points needed.

Does my nationality affect my points?

No. The system is nationality-blind by design — the same grid applies to applicants from every country. Points come from your qualification, experience and languages, never your passport.

Can I combine German and English points?

Partly. Only your highest German level counts (A2 = 1, B1 = 2, B2+ = 3 — not cumulative), and certified English at C1 adds 1 point on top. Strong English plus strong German is genuinely rewarded.

What are the baseline requirements besides points?

A recognised or partially recognised qualification, at least German A1 or English B2, and proof of funds of roughly €1,091 per month. Points alone are not enough.

Is this calculator official or legal advice?

Neither. It is a self-assessment aid built from the publicly documented points grid. Confirm your result with the official self-check on make-it-in-germany.com and consider a qualified immigration adviser for borderline cases.
