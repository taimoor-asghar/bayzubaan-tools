What a blocked account (Sperrkonto) actually is

A Sperrkonto is a special German bank account with a lock on it: you deposit a fixed sum before your visa appointment, and the money is released to you in monthly instalments after you arrive. Germany requires it as proof that you can support yourself — the logic is simple and unsentimental: a student who runs out of money in month three becomes a problem for the social system, so the state makes you lock the year's living costs away in advance. The account isn't a fee and it isn't lost money; it's your own money, paid back to you gradually. What stings is the timing: you need the full sum before you earn a single euro in Germany, at the exact moment you're also paying for flights, visas and deposits.

The requirement applies to non-EU nationals applying for the relevant residence visas — students, language learners, people getting foreign qualifications recognised, and Chancenkarte applicants. It applies the same way whether you're coming from Lahore, Lagos, Lima or Hanoi: the consulate cares about the visa category and the amount, not the passport. That neutrality is worth stating plainly, because rumours to the contrary circulate in every applicant community.

The 2026 amounts, explained

There are two rates, and which one applies depends on your visa category — not on your nationality, age or destination city:

Visa categoryMonthly rate (2026)12 months
Student visa (§16b AufenthG)€992€11,904
Language course (§16f), recognition (§16d), study-place search (§17), Chancenkarte (§20a)€1,091€13,092

The higher rate is the standard rate plus a 10% surcharge (€992 × 1.1 = €1,091.20, rounded to €1,091) that consulates apply to categories they consider less financially predictable. For stays shorter than a year, the maths is pro-rata: months × monthly rate. Six months on a student visa means 6 × €992 = €5,952 — but confirm with your consulate first, because not every consulate accepts less than 12 months, and some want the full year regardless of your plans.

One more thing the table doesn't show: these figures change almost every year, usually upward. The 2026 numbers were checked in October 2026 against provider and expat-guide sources. Before you transfer a cent, check your consulate's Merkblatt (information sheet) — the consulate's figure is the only one that counts at your appointment.

How it works, step by step

  1. Choose a provider and open the account online — before your visa appointment, from your home country. Common providers include Expatrio, Fintiba, Coracle and Deutsche Bank. Opening is fully remote; you'll need your passport and admission letter or relevant documents.
  2. Transfer the full blocked amount in one go. International transfers take days — start early, and keep the transfer receipt. Some providers also charge a setup fee (€50–150) and transfer fees on top; budget for those separately.
  3. Get the confirmation letter. The provider issues a blocking confirmation (Sperrbescheinigung) stating the amount and monthly payout. This letter goes to your visa appointment — without it, the appointment is usually wasted. Confirm before choosing a provider that your consulate accepts that provider's confirmation; consulates publish this in the Merkblatt and some are picky.
  4. Arrive, register, activate. After you land, do your Anmeldung (address registration — mandatory within 14 days), then activate the blocked account with the provider. The monthly payout (€992 or €1,091) starts flowing to your regular German current account. It is your living money now: rent, food, insurance, transport.

If the visa is refused, you can close the account and get the full amount refunded — providers handle this routinely against proof of refusal (the Ablehnungsbescheid). Ask about the exact refund process before transferring, not after.

The true cost: what the blocked amount doesn't cover

The blocked amount is the headline, but it is not the budget. The calculator above adds the one-time costs that ambush first-timers, all as editable estimates:

  • Visa fee (~€75) — paid at the appointment, non-refundable even if refused.
  • Provider setup fee (~€50–150) — varies by provider; ask for the all-in figure including transfer fees.
  • Incoming travel health insurance (~€30–50/month) — you need cover from the day you land until your regular German health insurance kicks in. Don't skip this: one emergency-room visit without insurance dwarfs every other cost on this list.
  • Document translations (~€20–40/page) — degrees, transcripts and certificates usually need certified translations. Count your pages before you budget.
  • Flight — wildly variable by route and season; enter your actual fare.
  • First-month buffer — German landlords ask for a deposit (Kaution), you need a SIM card, bedding, kitchen basics. Whatever you think you'll spend in week one, double it — everyone underestimates this.

Add it up and the real first-year number is typically €1,500–3,000 above the blocked amount, depending on your flight and how much deposit your landlord wants. Plan for it now, not at the airport.

Who doesn't need a Sperrkonto

Citizens of EU and EEA countries and Switzerland don't need a blocked account — or a visa at all — to live, study or work in Germany. That's EU freedom of movement, a legal fact, not a consulate favour. Everyone else applying for the visa categories above should assume the requirement applies to them, whatever their passport. If you're unsure whether your specific situation is exempt (scholarship holders, for example, sometimes prove funds differently), the answer is in your consulate's Merkblatt — not in a forum thread.

Choosing a provider without getting burned

Four names come up everywhere — Expatrio, Fintiba, Coracle and Deutsche Bank — and all four are legitimate. No ranking here, because the right choice depends on your consulate, not on a review score. The questions that actually matter: Does my consulate accept this provider's confirmation letter? (dealbreaker — check the Merkblatt), what is the all-in cost including setup and transfer fees, how fast does the confirmation arrive (you need it before the appointment), and how painless is the refund if the visa is refused. Open the account at least a month before your appointment; transfer delays are the classic reason appointments get wasted.

Timing: when to open and when to transfer

Open the blocked account at least a month before your visa appointment — the confirmation letter must exist before you walk in, and international transfers routinely take three to five working days, longer if your bank asks for source-of-funds documents. A common failure mode: the applicant transfers the money the week of the appointment, the transfer is still "in transit" on appointment day, and the appointment is wasted. Transfer early, keep every receipt, and screenshot the provider dashboard showing the received amount. After the visa is stamped, don't touch the account until you've done the Anmeldung — activation usually needs your German address registration and sometimes a video-ident step. Build a two-week buffer between arrival and the day you actually need the first payout to land.

Frequently asked questions

How much money do I need in a blocked account for Germany in 2026?

Student visa (§16b): €992 per month, i.e. €11,904 for 12 months. Language course (§16f), recognition (§16d), study-place search (§17) and Chancenkarte (§20a): €1,091 per month, i.e. €13,092 for 12 months. Shorter stays are pro-rata (months × rate), but confirm with your consulate — not all accept less than 12 months. Figures change yearly; the consulate's Merkblatt is authoritative.

Why is the blocked amount higher for language courses and the Chancenkarte?

A 10% surcharge is applied to the standard €992 rate for visa categories considered less financially predictable — language courses, recognition of qualifications, study-place search and the Chancenkarte — giving €1,091/month. Student visa holders pay the standard rate with no surcharge.

Do EU citizens need a Sperrkonto?

No. EU/EEA and Swiss citizens don't need a blocked account or a visa to live, study or work in Germany — that's EU freedom of movement. The requirement applies to non-EU nationals applying for the relevant residence visas.

When do I get the blocked money back?

Not as a lump sum: after arrival, Anmeldung and account activation, the provider pays it out monthly (€992 or €1,091 depending on your visa). It's your living money for the year, released gradually.

Which blocked account provider should I choose?

Common options: Expatrio, Fintiba, Coracle, Deutsche Bank. The decisive factor is consulate acceptance of the provider's confirmation letter — check your consulate's Merkblatt first. Then compare all-in fees, confirmation speed and the refund process.

What happens to my blocked account if my visa is refused?

You can close it and get the full amount refunded, usually against proof of refusal (the Ablehnungsbescheid). Ask your provider about their exact refund process and fees before you transfer.
