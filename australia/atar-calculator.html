How the ATAR is actually built

An ATAR is not a mark out of 100 and it is not a percentage. It is a percentile rank: an ATAR of 90.00 means you performed better than 90 percent of your age cohort, as measured through the admissions system, and an ATAR of 99.95 is the highest rank issued. That single fact explains most of the confusion around the number, because it means your ATAR depends on how everyone else performed as well as on how you performed, and it means the same set of marks can produce a different ATAR in a different year if the cohort changes. UAC, which issues ATARs for New South Wales and the Australian Capital Territory, states the mechanics plainly in its scaling reports, and this page follows those reports rather than any commercial rule of thumb.

The construction has three stages. First, the NSW Education Standards Authority reports your HSC marks, which describe the standard you reached in each course against published performance bands. Second, those marks are scaled. Scaling adjusts for the fact that different subjects attract candidatures of different overall academic strength, so that a student can be compared fairly across subject choices. A scaled mark answers the question of where you would have placed if the entire HSC cohort had taken that course, and it is reported on a one-unit basis out of 50. UAC is explicit that scaled marks are generally lower than HSC marks except near the top, and that there is no unique scaled mark for any given HSC mark, because the conversion runs through raw marks and moderated school assessments before alignment and rounding. Any calculator that promises an exact HSC-to-scaled conversion for a subject is therefore promising something UAC itself says does not exist, and this tool refuses to fake one.

Third, your aggregate is formed from your best two units of English plus your best eight other units, using scaled marks, for a maximum of 500. Note the 2025 change recorded in the preliminary scaling report: ATAR courses are no longer categorised as Category A or B, so the old restriction on counting former Category B courses no longer applies, and students counting more than two units of former Category B courses numbered 2,793 in 2025. Finally, the aggregate is converted to an ATAR using that year's published table, which is a rank table, not a formula. In 2025 the median ATAR was 70.75, 17.3 percent of students received 90.00 or above, and 53 students received 99.95. The table moves every year as participation rates and cohort composition shift, which is why this page stamps its year on every result and why last year's table is an estimate for this year's students, never a guarantee.

The most vivid illustration of why scaling matters comes from the 2025 scaling report itself. It describes two students, Liam and Kellie, each averaging exactly 40.0 marks per unit across five subjects and each sitting at the 50th percentile within every one of their own courses. Liam's subjects, including English Standard and Drama, carried an average scaled mean of 21.9 per unit, while Kellie's subjects, including English Advanced, Economics and French Continuers, carried an average scaled mean of 31.3. Liam received an ATAR of 60.00 and Kellie received 80.00, a twenty-point gap produced entirely by the strength of the candidatures they were ranked against. The lesson is not that students should chase supposedly high-scaling subjects regardless of ability — UAC's own data show that a Band 6 in a modestly scaling course beats a Band 4 in a strongly scaling one — but that subject choice genuinely moves the result, and that any honest estimator must show its scaling assumptions rather than hiding them inside a single magic number.

How to use this estimator without fooling yourself

Start from scaled marks if you have them, from a school estimate, a tutoring service that shows its tables, or a previous year's report. If you only have HSC marks, do not type them into the scaled-mark boxes: instead, form a rough aggregate by judgement, or use the published percentile information for your subjects to sense-check where you sit, and then use Option 1. Treat the output as a range, not a point: an estimate of 92.40 means roughly the low nineties on 2025 conversion, not a promise of 92.40 on results day. Remember also that course cut-offs, properly called selection ranks, are a separate matter from your ATAR; a university may add adjustment factors for a subject, a location or an equity scheme, and the published cut-off for a course in one year is the lowest selection rank that received an offer, not a fixed entry bar for the next year.

For students in other states, the structure is similar but the numbers are not interchangeable. Victoria's VTAC system works from scaled study scores with its own aggregate and its own published ATAR-to-aggregate table, Queensland, South Australia and Western Australia each have their own, and the aggregate that produces 95.00 in New South Wales will not produce 95.00 elsewhere. This page is NSW and UAC first by design, because that is the largest single cohort and the queue that ordered it, and it says so on every result rather than silently generalising. If you study outside New South Wales, use your own state's admissions centre tables and treat anything here as background on the concept only.

Frequently asked questions

Is this an official ATAR? No. This is an estimate only, from the published UAC 2025 table. Only UAC issues an official ATAR.

What is an aggregate? Your best 2 units of English plus your best 8 other units, in scaled marks out of 50 per unit, for a maximum of 500.

What is scaling? The adjustment that lets students be compared fairly across subjects with different candidatures. It is not a bonus for choosing a hard subject.

Why can two students with the same HSC marks get different ATARs? Because their subjects scaled differently. The UAC 2025 report's Liam and Kellie example shows identical average marks per unit producing ATARs of 60.00 and 80.00.

Which year's table does this calculator use? The UAC Preliminary Report on the Scaling of the 2025 NSW HSC, Table A9.

Does this cover states other than NSW? No. Victoria, Queensland, South Australia and Western Australia use different aggregates and tables.

What this tool cannot verify, stated openly: UAC publishes scaled marks as percentile distributions per subject (Table A3), not as a unique HSC-mark-to-scaled-mark conversion table, and it warns expressly against using Table A3 as a simple conversion table. This tool therefore does not invent per-subject conversion curves. It converts a genuine aggregate (or genuine scaled marks) to an estimated ATAR on the verified 2025 table, and it says so. A future version could add per-subject percentile interpolation for individual subjects once each subject's published percentile points are transcribed and checked, but that is not claimed here.
