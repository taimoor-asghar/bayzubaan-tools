What compound interest actually is

Compound interest is interest paid on interest. With simple interest, a deposit of $10,000 at 5 percent earns $500 every year, because the interest is always calculated on the original $10,000. With compound interest, the first year's $500 joins the principal, so the second year earns 5 percent of $10,500, which is $525, and the third year earns 5 percent of $11,025, which is $551.25. The yearly amounts grow even though the rate never changes, and that is the whole engine of long-term saving: time does more work than the rate does, because each year's growth becomes part of the base for every year after it. The standard formula captures this exactly: A = P(1 + r/n) raised to the power n times t, where P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is the number of compounding periods per year, and t is the number of years. A regular contribution adds a second stream, the future value of an annuity, which is why a modest monthly habit can matter more than a large one-off deposit made late.

Compounding frequency matters, but honestly, less than most marketing suggests. At 5 percent over ten years, $10,000 compounded annually reaches $16,288.95, while monthly compounding reaches $16,470.09, a difference of about $181 on a $6,289 gain. Daily compounding adds only a few dollars more. The gap widens at higher rates and longer horizons — at 12 percent over thirty years the frequency choice is worth real money — but for ordinary saving the dominant decisions are how much you put in, how early you start, and whether you keep contributing, not whether interest is credited monthly or quarterly. This calculator therefore lets you compare frequencies directly, but it refuses to present a frequency difference as if it were the main event; the year-by-year table shows the contribution stream doing the heavy lifting in most realistic plans.

The assumptions on this page are stated so that you can challenge them. Contributions are added at the end of each contribution period, which means a contribution starts earning interest from the next period onwards; a calculator that added contributions at the start of each period would show a slightly higher balance, and the difference is the interest on one period's contribution. The rate is fixed for the whole horizon, which no real investment delivers, and the result takes no account of inflation, which silently reduces what a future balance can buy, nor of taxes or fees, which reduce what you keep. A balance of $16,288.95 in ten years' time is not $16,288.95 of today's purchasing power if prices rose 2 percent a year in between; at that inflation rate it would buy roughly what $13,355 buys today. None of that makes the calculation useless — it makes it a measuring instrument for one variable, the growth of money at a stated rate, and like any instrument it should be read alongside the variables it does not measure.

Reading the table and the chart

The year-by-year table is the most useful output on this page, more useful than the headline balance, because it shows when growth changes character. In the early years the balance is mostly your own money; the interest column is small and the table looks almost linear. Somewhere in the middle, if the horizon is long enough, the interest column starts to grow faster than the contribution column, and from that point onwards the account is increasingly funding its own growth. That crossover is the moment compounding becomes visible, and it arrives later than most people expect — often well past the halfway mark of a twenty-year plan — which is why plans abandoned early never see it. The chart draws the same story: the dark lower portion is principal plus contributions, the lighter portion stacked on top is interest, and the widening of the lighter portion year by year is the compounding effect made visible. If your chart stays almost entirely dark, your plan is contribution-driven, and the honest lever is to contribute more or longer, not to hunt for a higher assumed rate.

A practical way to use the tool is to run three versions of the same plan: the plan as it stands, the plan with contributions starting five years earlier, and the plan with a rate one point lower than you hope for. The first comparison usually dwarfs the second, and the third is the reality check that stops a plan from depending on an optimistic rate. Run the same exercise for a debt in reverse and the lesson sharpens further: compound interest working against you, as on a credit card balance left unpaid, grows with exactly the same mathematics, and the same year-by-year table that looks encouraging for savings looks alarming for debt. The formula has no opinion about direction; it only rewards time, and time is the one input you cannot buy back.

Frequently asked questions

What is the compound interest formula? A = P(1 + r/n) raised to nt, where P is principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is compounding periods per year and t is years. Regular contributions add the future value of those payments.

How is this different from simple interest? Simple interest pays only on the original principal. Compound interest pays on principal plus accumulated interest, so growth accelerates.

Does compounding frequency matter much? Modestly at ordinary rates; the gap grows with the rate and the horizon. Contributions and time matter more.

Are contributions added before or after interest? At the end of each contribution period, so a contribution earns interest from the next period onwards.

Is this financial advice? No. It is an educational calculator at a fixed assumed rate. Real returns vary, and inflation, taxes and fees are not included.

What does daily compounding mean here? With no contributions, (1 + r/365) raised to 365t exactly. With contributions, the monthly simulation applies (1 + r/365) raised to 365/12 per month, as documented above.
